Steady rhythms for mood

For bipolar disorder and other mood problems that run in cycles. The body clock is one of the few levers you control directly, and a steadier clock means fewer and milder episodes.

Prepared by Alice Tran, PMHNP-BC, FNP-BC · Alice Tran Psychiatric Care · Updated October 2026

Read the full guide All patient resources

Why bother

In bipolar disorder, the brain's timing system is unusually sensitive. A few short nights can tip toward mania; a run of dim, late, irregular days can tip toward depression. The same sensitivity works in your favor: anchoring sleep, light, and meals to a fixed schedule is a treatment in its own right, with evidence comparable to a medication add-on. This handout is about building that anchor.

Evenings: getting to bed

  • Choose one bedtime, within thirty minutes, for all seven nights. Weekends included.
  • Dim the house two hours before bed. Lamps instead of overhead lights; warm bulbs; screens on night mode and held farther from the face, or off.
  • Stop work, exciting content, and arguments ninety minutes before bed. The brain needs a runway.
  • No caffeine after noon. No alcohol as a sleep aid; it fragments the second half of the night.
  • If you are not asleep after about twenty minutes, get up, sit in dim light, and return when drowsy. Lying awake teaches the bed to mean wakefulness.

Mornings: getting up

  • Choose one wake time and keep it every day, regardless of how the night went. The wake time is the anchor; bedtime follows from it.
  • Get bright light within thirty minutes of waking: outdoors for twenty minutes, or a light box, or at least the brightest room in the house with the curtains open.
  • Eat breakfast at a fixed time. Meals are a clock signal too.
  • Avoid naps longer than twenty minutes or after 3 p.m.
  • If getting up is the hardest part, a dawn-simulating alarm that brightens gradually over thirty minutes before the alarm sounds can help.

A sample day

Wake 7:00 with light and breakfast. Lunch 12:30. Exercise before 6 p.m. Dinner 6:30. Lights dimmed from 8:30. Screens off 9:30. Bed 10:30. The exact hours matter less than keeping the same ones every day.

Night owls and the seasons

If your natural rhythm runs late, move the wake time earlier by fifteen minutes every few days rather than all at once, and lean harder on morning light. In autumn and winter, when mornings are dark, a light box replaces the sun; in spring and early summer, when the days lengthen quickly, watch for shortened sleep and rising energy, and tighten the evening routine before an episode starts.

Light therapy and dark therapy

  • Light therapy for bipolar depression: a 10,000-lux box, about thirty minutes, usually at midday rather than early morning for people with bipolar disorder, and started at fifteen minutes. It must be coordinated with your prescriber, because too much or too early can tip toward mania.
  • Dark therapy for emerging mania or hypomania: extend the dark period to twelve or fourteen hours, in a dark room or with amber glasses that block blue light from early evening until morning. Several nights can slow a rising episode. Tell your prescriber the same day you start.

Cautions

  • Light therapy without your prescriber's knowledge, or any sudden burst of energy, reduced need for sleep, or racing thoughts: call us the same day
  • Two or more nights of very short sleep, or sleeping most of the day for more than a few days
  • Driving when drowsy
  • Thoughts of hurting yourself or not wanting to be here: call or text 988 any time, or go to the nearest emergency room. If you are in immediate danger, call 911.

Good to know

  • Shift work and frequent time-zone travel work against this treatment; if either is part of your life, tell us so the plan can be adjusted.
  • Track sleep and mood on one chart, by hand or in an app. The pattern is usually visible within a month.
  • Book: The Bipolar Disorder Survival Guide by David Miklowitz has a chapter on routine and sleep.
