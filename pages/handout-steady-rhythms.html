Steady rhythms for mood

Going to bed and getting up at the same times, keeping evenings dark, and getting bright light in the morning can steady mood the way a medication does, with almost no side effects. For bipolar and cyclic mood problems, alongside your medication.

Prepared by Alice Tran, PMHNP-BC, FNP-BC · Alice Tran Psychiatric Care · Updated October 2026

Read the full guide All patient resources

Why bother

In one study, people with bipolar disorder who were doing well were given either a sleep-focused therapy built around these steps or the same number of sessions of general mood education. Six months later, the sleep group had one-third the relapse rate. The biggest single ingredient is a steady daily rhythm, which helps on its own. Your body has a built-in clock. It may be running a little off, but you can train it: go to bed at the same time every night and get out of bed at the same time every morning, however the night went. Everything else below exists to make those two times easier to hit.

Evenings: getting to bed

  1. Pick one bedtime and hold it. Start with the bedtime you already hit most nights. Earlier than your set time is fine; later is not.
  2. Create darkness about two hours before bed, real or virtual. Only blue light carries the "daytime" signal. Amber blue-blocking glasses (at least 90 percent blockade; the inexpensive Uvex Skyper is the usual starting point) let you keep making lunches under the lights while your body clock reads the room as dark. Screen night-mode software does not remove enough blue.
  3. Make the bedroom dark and cool. People with very dark bedrooms had about half the rate of manic recurrence of people with light bedrooms. Blackout shades, or foil on the glass if you rent. Aim for 60 to 65°F; a fan helps.
  4. Build a wind-down ritual: tidy, a few pages of a calm book in dim light, pajamas, teeth, set the alarm, glasses off, lights off. The basics still apply: no big meals late, no hard exercise near bed, no caffeine after early afternoon, go easy on alcohol, no TV in the bedroom.

Mornings: getting up

  1. Get up at the same time, no matter how the night went. This is the harder half, and it does the most work, including after a rough night and on weekends. No snooze: get vertical and make the bed right away. Put the alarm across the room if you need to.
  2. Add light, movement, and people. Shoes on, open the front door, walk seven and a half minutes in any direction, and walk home. Then breakfast, and whatever puts you around people. Treat this as a direction, not a test you can fail.
  3. Get a dawn simulator. A bedside light that brightens from zero to full over the 30 minutes before your wake time, like a sunrise in your room. It cuts the groggy sleep-inertia feeling roughly in half and has an antidepressant effect in winter. Put it close to the bed; if it wakes you too early, start it later or dim it. For seasonal depression, start around September 1. If your room is blacked out, use it year-round.

A sample day

  • 6:15 am: dawn simulator starts while you sleep (thermostat warmed the room at 5:45). 6:45: out of bed, no snooze, make the bed. 7:15: shoes on, 7½ minutes out and back, then breakfast; in fall and winter, 30 to 60 minutes under a light box while you eat (at midday instead if you have bipolar disorder).
  • Early afternoon: last caffeine. No bright-light therapy after 2:30 pm.
  • 8:30 pm: blue-blockers on and lights down; the evening goes on as usual. 10:10: wind-down. 10:30: into a pitch-dark, cool room, glasses off. Same times tomorrow.

Night owls and the seasons

  • To shift earlier: move your evening darkness, bedtime, and rise time 5 minutes earlier every night or two. If mornings get harder, hold steady until getting up feels easy, then resume. The usual target is bed around 10:30 pm and up around 6:30 to 7:00 am.
  • Spring and summer, mania risk: keep your bedtime where it is as the days lengthen. The March light surge is a classic trigger; evening blue-blockers from late February help.
  • Fall and winter, depression risk: keep getting up at your usual time as mornings darken. A dawn simulator from September 1 keeps your "dawn" fixed all winter; a light box adds treatment if mood still dips.

Light therapy and dark therapy

  • Light therapy: 10,000 lux, a screen at least 12 by 17 inches (small boxes do not work), 30 to 60 minutes daily in the morning, above eye level at about 45 degrees, 12 to 17 inches away, with a UV filter. Read or eat under it; do not stare into it. Morning only; after about 2:30 pm it can flip the clock the wrong way. Expect benefit in 2 to 4 weeks. With bipolar disorder, use it at midday (12:00 to 2:30 pm), start at 15 minutes and add 15 minutes a week up to one hour, and cut back and call us if agitation, insomnia, racing thoughts, or irritability appear. Many people with bipolar disorder stop the box in February.
  • Dark therapy: enough darkness acts like a medication. In a hospital study, patients with mania who wore blue-blockers each evening improved significantly within a week and needed about half as much antipsychotic. For prevention, about 10 hours of darkness: glasses on 2 hours before bed, then 8 hours in a very dark room. For severe symptoms, about 14 hours, glasses on from 6 pm, moved later by an hour every two nights as you recover. Do not drive with the glasses on. Overnight, stay in the dark whether asleep or not.

Cautions

  • Do not do dark therapy on your own. Talk with us first; in the hospital study one patient's mania flipped quickly into depression, which is why someone should be watching alongside you.
  • Glasses worn before 6 pm, or during the day, can cause depressed mood. Vivid dreams and headaches are reported.
  • If you are still exhausted on a steady 8-hour schedule, ask about sleep apnea.
  • Sleep loss of several nights, feeling great on little sleep, or racing thoughts: call us the same day. Thoughts of hurting yourself or not wanting to be here: call or text 988 any time, or go to the nearest emergency room. If you are in immediate danger, call 911.
