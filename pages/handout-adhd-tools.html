ADHD: the top ten tools

Ten tools that are practical, fairly easy to set up, and make a big difference. Start with tools 1 and 2 and use them for a month. Then add one tool at a time, starting with whichever problem costs you the most.

Prepared by Alice Tran, PMHNP-BC, FNP-BC · Alice Tran Psychiatric Care · Updated October 2026

Read the full guide All patient resources

The idea behind every tool

ADHD gets described as trouble paying attention, but the part that wrecks an ordinary Tuesday is executive function: holding information in mind, keeping track of time, getting started, picking what matters, switching gears, settling reactions. Brain-training games do not carry over to daily life. What works is moving the job out of your head: remembering goes to a calendar and a task list, keeping time goes to timers, deciding goes to a few rules you set once. Every tool has to pass one test: will you see it at the moment you need it?

Tools 1 and 2: one calendar, one task list

  • The calendar holds everything with a set time. The task list holds everything else. A report due Friday needs both: a dated task and a calendar block to do it.
  • Golden rule 1: one system only. Work and personal together. No sticky notes, no second notebook, no "I'll remember that one."
  • Golden rule 2: capture everything immediately, in 3 to 5 words, while the other person is still talking if necessary. "I'll add it later" means never.
  • Daily review, 2 to 5 minutes every morning: open today's calendar with the list beside it, clear yesterday, letter anything new, give your A tasks a time slot, hardest one in your best-focus hours, and leave one gap empty.
  • Weekly review, 15 minutes: next week's prep, two to four weeks out, re-letter, move stale C tasks to a long-term list.
  • Pick one system and use it for 30 days before judging it. Google Calendar plus Google Tasks, Apple Calendar plus Reminders, Outlook plus To Do, or a paper planner all work. When you fall off, do not back-fill: triage the overdue pile, brain dump for 15 minutes, and do a normal review tomorrow.

Tool 3: ABC priorities

  • Letter every task when you add it. A: must happen today or tomorrow; there is a real consequence if it waits. B: important, and this week is soon enough. C: needs doing, low stakes. Long-term list: no deadline, will not happen in the next two weeks.
  • Ask in order and stop at the first yes: real consequence past tomorrow? Needs to happen this week? Realistically in the next two weeks?
  • On a normal workday, one to three A tasks is plenty. If everything is an A, ask literally what happens if it waits until the day after tomorrow.

Tool 4: break big tasks down

  • Starting is the hardest part, and a big task gives you nothing to start on. A good subtask starts with a verb, you can picture yourself doing it, and the first one takes under five minutes: "Find last year's tax return," not "Taxes."
  • Break down any task over about an hour, and any task that has rolled over three days in a row. If breaking it down feels like too much, the only subtask is "spend 10 minutes listing the steps."
  • Time estimates run short with ADHD. Double your first guess and check afterward.

Tools 5 and 6: timed bursts and the distraction log

  • Pomodoro: pick one task, phone in another room, timer for 25 minutes, work on that only. When it rings, stop and take 5 minutes, with a timer for the break too. After four rounds, 15 to 30 minutes. Try 15 on 3 off for boring tasks and 45 on 10 off for deep work. Two rounds in a day is a real win.
  • Distraction log: a paper pad beside you. When an unrelated thought barges in, write 3 to 5 words and go straight back. Process the log at the break: most is trash, some becomes tasks with a letter, anything under two minutes gets done in the break.

Tool 7: systems for your stuff

  • Landing station: one spot by the door with a charger, a tray for keys and wallet, and a hook for the bag. The moment you walk in, everything goes there before anything else. Practice it deliberately for two weeks, and ask the people you live with to return strays to it.
  • Phone-wallet-key: a phone case that holds your cards, with the key inside or clipped to it. One thing to remember, and it is the thing you already check dozens of times a day.
  • Backups: spare keys with a trusted neighbor or a lockbox (never under the mat), and a Bluetooth tracker on every bag and key ring.

Tool 8: automatic payments

  • Turn on autopay the moment you sign up for anything recurring: credit cards (at least the minimum; the full balance if you can), utilities, phone, insurance, rent, loans. Pick payment dates a few days after payday and keep a cushion in checking with low-balance alerts.
  • Add a monthly 10-minute "glance at statements" event, because autopay fails silently when a card expires.
  • Twice a year, 30 minutes: find and cancel subscriptions you no longer use.

Tool 9: simplify your environment

  • Clear the desk to what today's task needs. Face a wall. Phone out of arm's reach, ideally another room.
  • Turn off every notification except calls, texts, calendar, and money or safety alerts. Use a Focus mode for work blocks.
  • Make your phone grayscale, or at least tinted icons. It is disorienting for a week and then changes which apps you open.
  • Delete the endless-scroll apps, or set app timers with someone else holding the passcode.
  • Unsubscribe from newsletters and promotions in one pass.

Tool 10: your thoughts

  • Many adults with ADHD grew up being told they were lazy, and the harsh voice stuck. It grows out of years of criticism; it is not a symptom, so you can work on it.
  • Replacement thoughts (CBT): catch the thought, rate how much you believe it, name the thinking error (labeling, all-or-nothing, fortune-telling, "should" statements), then write a replacement that is 100 percent true and strong enough to take over. "I always mess things up" becomes "I missed this deadline and need to tell my manager and fix it. I've hit most of my deadlines this year, and my last review was good."
  • Getting distance (ACT): "I'm a failure" becomes "I'm having the thought that I'm a failure," then "I'm noticing that I'm having the thought that I'm a failure." Same words, less grip. Some people picture the inner critic with a silly face filter: "oh, it's him again."

Do this today

  1. Install a calendar and a task app, put the task widget on your first home screen, and enter the next three things you need to do.
  2. Put a 45-minute "brain dump" on the calendar for this week: every obligation out of your head and into the system.
  3. Pick a landing station spot and put a charger and a bowl there.
  4. Turn on autopay for one bill tonight.
  5. Turn off every notification except calls, messages, and calendar.

Get help right away if

  • You cannot manage even 15 minutes of focus most days: tell us, because this is what ADHD treatment is meant to help
  • Worries crowd out work most days: anxiety often travels with ADHD and is treatable too
  • Thoughts of hurting yourself or not wanting to be here: call or text 988 any time, or go to the nearest emergency room. If you are in immediate danger, call 911.

Good to know

  • These tools work alongside medication and therapy, not instead of them. Medication makes the tools easier to use; it does not replace them.
  • Books: Mastering Your Adult ADHD: Client Workbook by Safren and colleagues; Feeling Great by David Burns for the thought skills. The ADHD handout on this site covers diagnosis and treatment.
