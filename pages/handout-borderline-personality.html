Borderline personality

A diagnosis, not an identity, and one that many people recover from. This page explains what it is, what helps, and how to choose a therapist.

Prepared by Alice Tran, PMHNP-BC, FNP-BC · Alice Tran Psychiatric Care · Updated October 2026

Read the full guide All patient resources

What it is

BPD is a pattern of intense, unstable relationships, fast mood shifts tied to what happens between people, an unstable sense of self, emptiness, impulsive behavior, and sometimes self-harm. Fear of abandonment drives much of it. It comes from a mix of temperament, attachment, trauma, and invalidating environments. No single experience is required.

What it is not

It is not a judgment of character. People with BPD want to get better and do not know why they are suffering. It was once called untreatable, and that is inaccurate: many people improve, especially with structured treatment. Two things are both true: the natural course tends to improve, and you should not wait for it.

Treatment has three parts

  • Therapy: a structured psychotherapy is the main treatment, such as DBT (dialectical behavior therapy) or mentalization-based therapy. Weekly for moderate to severe.
  • Skills: practice outside a crisis. Judge progress over months, not hours.
  • Medication: a helper for depression, anxiety, impulsivity, or anger. It does not reach the core, and none is approved for BPD. A life outside therapy is part of the treatment: stable work, relationships, and routines.

Learn your pattern

Event, interpretation, emotion, urge, action, consequence. The goal is to notice the sequence and make more room before acting. Make a plan: "When I notice __, I will try __ before I __." For example: wait 15 minutes and write down what I fear before sending another message.

Radical acceptance

Pain plus fighting reality equals suffering. Catch the "should." State the facts and what caused them. Accept with your body: open hands, dropped shoulders, half-smile. Act as if you have accepted. Try "I hate ___, AND ___ is the reality right now." Acceptance is not approval, and it never means staying in harm's way. The radical acceptance handout on this site walks through the four moves.

Practice list

Check one or two to start with, not all ten.

  1. Map one hard interaction: feeling, story, urge, action.
  2. Rate the feeling 0 to 10 and write two other explanations.
  3. Practice skills when calm, not only in a crisis.
  4. Try DBT mindfulness: observe, describe, participate.
  5. Wait 15 minutes before sending the message.
  6. Write down what you fear before you act on it.
  7. Write a crisis plan while you are well.
  8. Bring disagreements about treatment into the room.
  9. Build stable work, relationships, and routines.
  10. Try radical acceptance on a small problem first.

Choosing a therapist

  • A clear framework and goals
  • A crisis plan and support between sessions
  • A plan for missed sessions and disagreements
  • Progress you can track
  • Coordination with your prescriber

Get help right away if

  • Self-harm urges or thoughts of suicide can come on fast. Use your crisis plan.
  • Any self-harm that needs medical care, or an overdose, even a small one: go to the emergency room
  • Several days without sleep, or substance use that is escalating
  • Thoughts of hurting yourself or not wanting to be here: call or text 988 any time, or go to the nearest emergency room. If you are in immediate danger, call 911.

Good to know

  • Books: The Dialectical Behavior Therapy Skills Workbook by McKay, Wood, and Brantley; DBT Skills Training Handouts and Worksheets by Marsha Linehan; Radical Acceptance by Tara Brach.
