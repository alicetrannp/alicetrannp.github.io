Social anxiety

You have been diagnosed with social anxiety disorder. This page explains why the fear of being judged keeps coming back, and the tools that make it smaller.

Prepared by Alice Tran, PMHNP-BC, FNP-BC · Alice Tran Psychiatric Care · Updated October 2026

Read the full guide All patient resources

What it is

Almost everyone gets nervous before a speech or a first date. Social anxiety is when the fear of embarrassment or rejection makes you avoid ordinary situations, or get through them with dread, for months at a time, and it starts to cost you at work, with friends, or in dating. Some people only fear performing; others feel it in almost any conversation. You are not weak, and nothing is wrong with who you are. It is very treatable.

What happens when you feel watched

Your body's alarm reacts to the chance of being judged the way it would to a bear: hot face, pounding heart, knotted stomach, shaky hands and voice. None of it is dangerous. The trouble is where your attention goes. During a conversation it pulls inward, checking your face, your voice, your words, and that builds a picture of how you look that is far worse than what anyone sees. Watching yourself also means you miss the nod or the smile in front of you, and the feelings you watch get stronger.

Why it keeps coming back

Skipping an event brings relief, and relief teaches your brain that going was dangerous. Going but hiding works the same way: rehearsing every sentence, short answers, no eye contact, hands busy with a drink, an excuse to leave early. When the conversation goes fine, your brain credits the hiding, and other people can see these habits, so you can come across as cold when you want to connect. Afterward comes the replay, built from the anxious picture, so each pass looks worse than it was.

Treatment has three parts

  • Exposure: go into the situations you have been avoiding, easiest first, and stay long enough to see what happens when you do not hide. Reassurance does not do much; seeing for yourself does.
  • Attention training and dropping safety behaviors: the five tools below.
  • Medication: an SSRI such as sertraline or escitalopram when anxiety is severe or therapy alone is not enough; propranolol before a specific performance. Medication helps, but it does not replace practice.

Build your fear ladder

  1. List 10 to 15 situations you avoid and the safety behaviors you use to get through the rest. Be specific: "eat lunch in the break room" rather than "work stuff."
  2. Rate each from 0 to 100 for how anxious you would feel doing it without the safety behaviors.
  3. Sort from easiest to hardest. If there is a big jump, add a step between by changing one thing: how long you stay, who comes, how many people.
  4. Start at a step rated 30 to 50. Before: write what you fear, in a form you could check, and how sure you are. During: stay, drop the safety behaviors, describe the feelings plainly. After: write what a camera would have recorded, re-rate your fear, and note what you learned.
  5. Practice most days, at least three or four times a week, until the step is about half as hard. Then move up, and vary the people, places, and times.

Five tools

  • Move your attention outward. Five minutes a day, put all your attention on a podcast voice; when it drifts inward, say "inward" and bring it back. In conversations, give yourself an outward job: learn one specific thing about each person.
  • Drop the safety behaviors that backfire. One conversation with no rehearsing. Look at the speaker's face. Add one sentence to every answer. Keep your hands free for a whole event. Share one opinion instead of asking question after question.
  • Check the picture against a video. Predict how anxious you will look from 0 to 100, record a short practice talk, watch it the next day as if it were a stranger, and compare.
  • Make small mistakes on purpose. Send a message with a typo and do not correct it; pause five seconds mid-sentence; ask for directions to a place you are standing next to. Write what you expect people to do and what they did.
  • Put a time limit on the replay. Write three lines: what happened, what I fear they thought, what evidence I have. Fix anything real, put the rest on a worry list for a daily ten-minute worry time, and bring attention back outward.

Habits that help

Check one or two to start with, not all ten.

  1. Do the five-minute outward-listening practice every day for two weeks.
  2. Say one sentence to a coworker that you do not need to say.
  3. Eat lunch where other people are.
  4. Call a business with a question instead of looking it up.
  5. Keep alcohol out of practice steps, so your brain does not credit the drink.
  6. Schedule the next three practices on your calendar.
  7. After a hard day, go back down one step for two or three practices, then climb again.
  8. Fade out a support person in stages.
  9. Keep caffeine moderate; it mimics the body signs you fear.
  10. Tell one person what you are working on, so avoidance has a witness.

If it is not getting easier

  • A safety behavior is sneaking in, such as rehearsing in your head or only going with a certain friend. Find it and drop it.
  • The step is too big. Do a smaller version twice, then return.
  • You are not practicing often enough. Put the exact day and time on the calendar.

Get help right away if

  • Anxiety so severe you cannot work, attend school, or leave the house
  • Heavy daily drinking to get through social situations; do not stop suddenly on your own, since withdrawal can be dangerous, and tell us
  • New or worsening thoughts of hurting yourself, especially in the first weeks of a new medication
  • Thoughts of hurting yourself or not wanting to be here: call or text 988 any time, or go to the nearest emergency room. If you are in immediate danger, call 911.

Good to know

  • Progress is not a straight line. Fear comes back a little after a break or a stressful stretch; it does not erase what you learned.
  • Judge each practice by what you did, not by how anxious you felt. In a short conversation the anxiety may not have time to come down, and the practice still counts.
  • Workbook: Managing Social Anxiety: Workbook by Hope, Heimberg, and Turk. App: MindShift CBT (free).
