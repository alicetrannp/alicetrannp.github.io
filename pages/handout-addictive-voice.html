The addictive voice

One skill: learning to hear the part of your mind that argues for using as a voice that is not you. Where it says "addiction," read your own: alcohol, drugs, vaping, gambling, pornography. The technique does not care which.

Prepared by Alice Tran, PMHNP-BC, FNP-BC · Alice Tran Psychiatric Care · Updated October 2026

Read the full guide All patient resources

A thirty-second experiment

Think seriously about never using again, for the rest of your life. Not cutting back, not a break. Hold that for half a minute and notice what happens. You probably felt two things at once: a flicker of hope and relief at the life you want, and a lurch, a sinking, almost panicky recoil at the word "never." The usual name for this is ambivalence. AVRT rejects that framing: you want to quit, and the hope was you. The recoil came from somewhere else, and AVRT calls it the addictive voice.

What the addictive voice is

Any thought or feeling that supports, suggests, or leaves open the possibility of ever using again. The obvious thoughts count, and so do the ones that keep the door open a crack: "maybe someday, at a wedding," "I'll quit after this stressful month," "who can really say never?" In a simplified picture, the newer brain handles language, values, plans, and everything you mean by "me." The older brain runs appetite, and an addiction has hijacked it with a pleasure bigger than anything nature intended. Two things follow: wanting to use does not make you sick or weak, and the desire is not you.

How the voice disguises itself

  • It speaks as "I," in your own vocabulary and sense of humor. The technique is catching the sentence and correcting the pronoun: "I want to use" becomes "it wants to use."
  • Its greatest hits sound prudent: "Saying never is setting myself up to fail." "Plenty of studies show people learn to moderate." "This is a terrible month to take on something this big." "Nothing that bad has happened yet." "I'll do six months and reassess." "I have a disease; expecting myself never to relapse is unrealistic."
  • Run each through the definition: does it leave the door open to using in the future? If so, it is the voice, however sensible the costume. They say later, maybe, or someday, and none of them says never.

The one thing it cannot do

However loud it gets, the addictive voice cannot move a muscle. It cannot pour a drink, light anything, type a URL, or drive to the store. All it can do is want, and wanting has no hands. Wiggle your index finger. Now invite the desire to do the same without your permission. It cannot. Every time you have ever used, it needed your muscles and your consent. A desire can want something forever and still do nothing without you.

The Big Plan

AVRT's commitment is a one-time, permanent decision, deliberately more absolute than "I'll try" or "one day at a time": "I will never use again, and I will never change my mind." If you feel dread or angry static as you say it, that is the voice hearing the one word it fears. To the appetite, "probably never" is a completely different sentence from "never." As long as the option survives anywhere in your future, the voice has a job: wear you down and pick its moment. Close the option completely and the job is gone. It also has to be now; the appetite has no calendar, and January will feel exactly as hard as today. There is no small use: one puff, one sip, or one bet breaks the plan, and the voice will be waiting with "see, you're powerless, might as well go all the way." If that happens, do not accept the relapse script. Treat it as a serious broken promise, work out how the voice engineered it, and remake the plan more firmly.

After the plan: recognize, do not argue

  • Urges will still come, sometimes vivid, sometimes months later out of nowhere. Recognize it as the addictive voice, label it, and go about your day.
  • Do not debate it. Once you start arguing, you have agreed to a negotiation, and negotiating with an appetite can go on all night. You owe the voice no answer.
  • If it is loud, change the channel physically: leave the room, take a walk, call someone, the way you would excuse yourself from a conversation you do not want to be in.
  • Expect the noise to taper over weeks to months, with occasional spikes. A spike is a strange kind of good news: you are hearing the appetite clearly from the outside.

Where this fits

AVRT comes from Rational Recovery, and in its orthodox form it rejects the disease model, support groups, and treatment of any kind. Our view is broader. Take the core skill, hearing the addictive voice as not-you and closing the door completely, and leave the turf war behind. Medications such as naltrexone or buprenorphine, therapy, and recovery communities do not compete with your Big Plan, and for many people they are what makes it stick.

Before you quit cold turkey, read this

  • Heavy daily alcohol use and benzodiazepines (Xanax, Klonopin, Ativan) can cause withdrawal seizures and, with alcohol, delirium tremens, which can be fatal. Your decision can be absolute today while the stopping is done with medical supervision and possibly a taper. That is not the addictive voice talking; that is physiology. Call us first.
  • Opioids: after any period of abstinence your tolerance drops, and a return to the old dose can be a fatal overdose. One more reason to take "no small use" seriously, and to have a clinician and naloxone in the picture.
  • Thoughts of hurting yourself or not wanting to be here: call or text 988 any time, or go to the nearest emergency room. If you are in immediate danger, call 911.
