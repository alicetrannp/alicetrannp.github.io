Anxiety as a habit

Anxiety is a feeling, and worry is what you do about it. Worrying can turn into a habit that keeps the anxiety coming back. This page explains the loop and what to do when you catch yourself in it.

Prepared by Alice Tran, PMHNP-BC, FNP-BC · Alice Tran Psychiatric Care · Updated October 2026

Read the full guide All patient resources

Anxiety and worry are two different things

Anxiety is a feeling: unease about something uncertain or still to come, noticed in the body as a tight chest, a knot in the stomach, restlessness, a sense that something has to be done now. Worry is something you do: the thinking that follows the feeling, going over what might happen, picturing the worst, rehearsing how you would handle it. A feeling arrives on its own. Worrying is a response, and responses can change with practice.

The worry loop

  • Trigger: a feeling of anxiety or uncertainty, or a thought about something that could go wrong.
  • Worry: replaying possibilities, imagining worst cases, analyzing, rehearsing.
  • Reward: worrying feels productive. It gives you something to do and brings a short sense of relief or control.
  • The relief fades, the anxiety comes back, and that becomes the trigger for the next round. Each pass teaches your brain that worrying is what to do when things feel uncertain.

Why worrying feels like it helps

Anxiety comes with an uncomfortable push to do something. When the problem is right in front of you, doing something is right. Most anxiety is about the future, which cannot be solved today, so the push has nowhere to go, and the mind finds it a job: worrying. Feeling better for a minute is not the same as solving anything, and that gap keeps the habit going.

When you catch yourself worrying

  1. Notice it. Name it, silently or out loud: "I'm feeling uncertain, and my brain is starting to worry." Naming puts a little space between you and the habit.
  2. Get curious. What set this off? What am I doing in response? Is the worry helping me solve anything today?
  3. Act or allow. If there is a real step you can take, take it or put it on the calendar. If there is nothing to do yet, let the uncertainty sit there without trying to think your way out of it.
  4. Come back. Notice how the anxiety feels in your body, with interest instead of alarm, then bring your attention back to what is in front of you.

Habits that help

Check one or two to start with, not all ten.

  1. Ask of each worry: can I do something about this in the next 48 hours? Yes means plan the first step; no means it waits for worry time.
  2. Keep a daily worry time, 15 to 30 minutes, same time and place, not in bed, with a timer.
  3. Keep a worry list on your phone and tell each worry "not now, at 6:30."
  4. Solve problems on paper: one sentence, five options, pick one, plan the first step, do it, review.
  5. Check worry's track record: write what you fear and how likely it feels, then come back days later and write what happened.
  6. Practice not knowing: pick a restaurant without reading reviews, send an email after one read, go out without checking the weather.
  7. Turn a what-if into a prediction you can rate, then ask how likely it is and how you would cope.
  8. Talk to yourself like a good coach, not a drill sergeant.
  9. Five minutes a day of slow breathing with a long breath out.
  10. Cut caffeine after noon and keep the phone out of the bedroom.

When worry is more than a habit

Seeing anxiety as a habit fits many people, and it is not the whole picture for everyone. Anxiety disorders are real and treatable, and therapy, medication, or both help many people. If worry is taking over your days, sleep, or work, or comes with panic attacks, a past trauma, or a low mood that will not lift, bring it to your visit rather than managing it alone.

Get help right away if

  • Worry that keeps you from working, sleeping, or caring for yourself
  • Panic attacks, nightmares or flashbacks, or a low mood that will not lift
  • Thoughts of hurting yourself or not wanting to be here: call or text 988 any time, or go to the nearest emergency room. If you are in immediate danger, call 911.

Good to know

  • Expect to repeat the four steps many times. Over time you are teaching your brain a different response: uncertainty does not have to mean worry.
  • This handout draws on the work of psychiatrist Jud Brewer on anxiety as a habit loop and psychologist Thomas Borkovec on worry.
  • See also the anxiety handout on this site, which covers exposure and the fear ladder.
