Anxiety as a habit

Anxiety is the feeling in your body. Worry is the mental activity you add to it, and with enough repetition that activity starts running on its own. This page describes the loop and how to step out of it.

Prepared by Alice Tran, PMHNP-BC, FNP-BC · Alice Tran Psychiatric Care · Updated October 2026

Read the full guide All patient resources

Anxiety and worry are two different things

Anxiety is a body state: tight chest, restless stomach, a sense that something is wrong. Worry is a mental activity: running scenarios, planning for the worst, re-checking. The feeling arrives on its own. The worrying is a response, and responses can be practiced or unpracticed. Many people who describe themselves as anxious are, more precisely, skilled worriers whose skill has become automatic.

The worry loop

  1. A trigger: an email, a symptom, a silence from someone.
  2. The anxious feeling.
  3. Worrying, which feels like doing something about it.
  4. A small drop in the feeling, because attention has moved from the body to the thinking.
  5. The brain notes: worrying helped. Next time the feeling comes, it reaches for worry faster.

Why worrying feels like it helps

  • It feels like preparation, though the same scenario is usually rehearsed dozens of times without producing a plan.
  • It feels like control, because thinking is an activity and activity feels better than waiting.
  • It feels responsible, as if the people who do not worry are careless.
  • It occasionally coincides with a good outcome, which it then takes credit for.
  • The relief is real, but brief, and the price is a brain increasingly tuned to threats.

When you catch yourself worrying

  • Name it: "I am worrying." This is different from "I am thinking about the problem."
  • Ask one question: "Is there a specific action I can take in the next hour?" If yes, do it or schedule it. If no, the worry has nothing to work with.
  • Write the topic on a worry list and leave it there until a daily worry period of fifteen to twenty minutes, not at bedtime.
  • Return attention to the body and the present task. Notice the feeling without feeding it. It will fade on its own if you let it.
  • Expect to catch yourself many times a day at first. Each catch is a repetition of the new habit.

Habits that help

Check one or two to start with, not all ten.

  1. Keep a worry list in your phone and a fixed daily time to review it.
  2. Exercise most days; it burns off the physical charge that worry feeds on.
  3. Keep caffeine moderate.
  4. Put a time limit on news and social media.
  5. Practice tolerating one small uncertainty a day without checking: an unread email, an unanswered text.
  6. Notice when you ask for reassurance, and try waiting an hour first.
  7. Spend ten minutes daily on a breathing or mindfulness exercise, as training rather than rescue.
  8. Protect sleep; a tired brain worries more.
  9. When a worry is about something real and actionable, write a two-line plan and stop there.
  10. Tell one person what you are practicing.

When worry is more than a habit

Worry that fills most days for six months or more, with poor sleep, muscle tension, and trouble concentrating, is generalized anxiety disorder. Worry fixed on one theme, such as health, social judgment, or intrusive thoughts, points to a specific anxiety disorder or OCD. All of these respond to cognitive behavioral therapy, and SSRIs help when the volume is too high to practice. The habit skills above still apply; they simply need support.

Get help right away if

  • Anxiety that prevents work, school, or leaving home
  • A first episode of racing heart, chest pain, or breathlessness that has not been medically evaluated
  • New or worsening thoughts of self-harm, especially in the first weeks of a new medication
  • Thoughts of hurting yourself or not wanting to be here: call or text 988 any time, or go to the nearest emergency room. If you are in immediate danger, call 911.

Good to know

  • Worry declines gradually, not all at once. Count the minutes you spend worrying per day; the number falls before the feeling does.
  • Book: The Worry Cure by Robert Leahy.
