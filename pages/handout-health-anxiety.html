Health anxiety

Worry about your health has taken over, even after normal results. This page explains why the fear keeps coming back, how to make it smaller, and how to tell when a symptom does need a doctor.

Prepared by Alice Tran, PMHNP-BC, FNP-BC · Alice Tran Psychiatric Care · Updated October 2026

Read the full guide All patient resources

What it is

Everyone worries about their health now and then. Health anxiety is when you cannot stop: the fear stays after a normal exam or normal tests, and checking your body, searching your symptoms, and asking people whether you look okay take up hours of your week. The feelings in your body are real. What keeps the fear going is the scary meaning your mind gives them and the checking that follows, and both can change with practice.

The checking cycle

Your body makes small sensations all the time. With health anxiety, your mind jumps to the scariest explanation, which sets off the alarm, and adrenaline then creates new sensations that look like more proof. Watching one body part closely makes you notice more there. Checking, searching, or asking brings relief, then doubt creeps back, and you watch your body even more closely. Checking also backfires directly: pressing a spot makes it sore, and online searches put rare, frightening causes at the top.

Why reassurance does not last

Being told you are fine does not switch the alarm off, because knowing a fact and feeling safe are different things. No test can rule out every illness you might get someday, and as long as you need that promise, the worry will keep asking for one more check. What helps is getting more comfortable with not knowing for sure, a little at a time.

Build your fear ladder

  1. List what you avoid (hospital shows, waiting rooms, news stories) and what you check. Be specific.
  2. Rate each from 0 to 100 for how anxious you would feel doing it without checking or asking afterward.
  3. Sort from easiest to hardest and start at a step rated 30 to 50.
  4. Before each practice, write what you fear will happen and how sure you are. During, stay with it and skip the checking. After, write what happened, re-rate, and note what you learned.
  5. For fears you cannot practice in real life, write a page about the moment you fear, in the present tense, with no happy ending, and read it aloud 20 to 30 minutes a day until it is boring. If it would bring back a frightening medical event that really happened, do this step with a therapist.

Five tools

  • One appointment for each new symptom. Get a new symptom checked once if it lasts two to three weeks or is on the warning list below. Tell the doctor what you are afraid it is. Accept the plan, and do not seek another opinion unless something changes.
  • Stop searching your symptoms. Delete health bookmarks and symptom-checker apps; block the sites you use most; write the worry in a note instead and save it for worry time.
  • Cut back on checking, a little at a time. Count for two days, then shrink each kind of checking week by week: ten presses a day becomes five at set times, then two, then one, then none. Checks your doctor asked you to do stay.
  • Cut back on reassurance. Agree with the person you ask, at a calm moment, on a shrinking daily budget and a script: "I love you, and I'm not answering that one, because we agreed answering makes it worse. I'll sit with you while it passes."
  • Make a plan with your doctor. Ask for a regular check-in every few months instead of a visit for every new worry, and ask which specific symptoms should make you call before then.

Habits that help

Check one or two to start with, not all ten.

  1. Delete the symptom-checker app today.
  2. Watch one episode of a hospital drama without searching afterward.
  3. Go 24 hours without pressing, poking, or checking the mirror.
  4. Keep a tally of checks for two days and plan the cut-back.
  5. Write the worry down instead of searching it.
  6. Use a daily worry time, 15 to 30 minutes, not in bed.
  7. Notice a small new ache and wait two to three weeks, with no searching, before deciding whether to call (warning signs are the exception).
  8. Sit in a waiting room for 20 minutes with your phone in your bag.
  9. Exercise most days, so a fast heart and heavy breathing become familiar rather than alarming.
  10. Keep caffeine moderate; it causes the palpitations and jitters that start the cycle.

Symptoms that do need attention

  • Same day: coughing up or vomiting blood; black or bloody stools.
  • Book an appointment: weight loss you cannot explain, repeated fevers, or drenching night sweats; a lump that is growing, feels hard, or is still there after two to three weeks; any new symptom that keeps getting worse or lasts more than two to three weeks.
  • Also act on anything your doctor has specifically told you to watch for. Most other new symptoms can wait two to three weeks, and if still there, book a visit.

Get help right away if

  • A first episode of symptoms a doctor has never looked at: get checked once, because thyroid, heart rhythm, asthma, low blood sugar, decongestants, steroids, stimulants, and alcohol or sedative withdrawal can all feel like anxiety
  • Anxiety so severe you cannot work or leave the house
  • Thoughts of hurting yourself or not wanting to be here: call or text 988 any time, or go to the nearest emergency room. If you are in immediate danger, call 911.

Good to know

  • Expect your anxiety to go up at first when you cut back on checking. That means it is working, and it comes down if you stick with it.
  • Medication: an SSRI can lower the overall volume of worry when it is severe; it works best alongside the tools above, not instead of them.
  • Book: It's Not All in Your Head by Asmundson and Taylor.
