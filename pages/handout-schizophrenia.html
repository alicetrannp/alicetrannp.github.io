Schizophrenia

For patients and families. This page explains what schizophrenia is, what helps, and how families can stay allies.

Prepared by Alice Tran, PMHNP-BC, FNP-BC · Alice Tran Psychiatric Care · Updated October 2026

Read the full guide All patient resources

What it is

Schizophrenia is a group of symptoms: hearing or seeing things others do not, fixed beliefs others do not share, disorganized thinking, loss of drive, and trouble with focus and memory. Together they affect how well you can work, study, and connect. Treatment aims to restore that, not just quiet voices. One way to understand it: the brain tags ordinary things as important, then builds a story to explain why.

Insight, and staying hopeful

Low insight is part of the illness, not stubbornness. The goal is not to get anyone to say "I have schizophrenia." Someone who says "I don't have it, but I take my medication every day" does better than someone who accepts the label and stops. Stay hopeful and realistic: medication works well, though it is not fun.

Treatment has three parts

  • Medication: it keeps you out of the hospital. Report sleepiness, restlessness, or weight gain early, since all three can be managed. Ask whether a long-acting injection could suit you. If other medicines have not worked well, ask about clozapine.
  • Body and routine: steady sleep, a fixed wake-up, regular movement, and health checks protect your mind and your heart.
  • Support: family education, coping tools like headphones and music, and a notebook of voices and what helped.

Taking medication every day

Staying on medication is the biggest thing within your control.

  • Fill a weekly organizer each Sunday, with someone if you can.
  • Use an alarm or a smart dispenser that alerts family to a missed dose.
  • Ask about a once-daily dose at night.
  • Ask about a monthly or six-monthly injection.

Protect your body

Sleep loss and flare-ups travel together. Alcohol and cannabis disrupt sleep, and cannabis and stimulants such as methamphetamine can push vulnerable people toward lasting psychosis. Heart health matters: antipsychotics can raise weight, blood sugar, and cholesterol, so weigh yourself regularly, check blood pressure, and keep the lab appointments. On clozapine, tell us before you quit smoking, since quitting raises the level.

Daily habits

Check one or two to start with, not all ten.

  1. Take medication at the same time every day.
  2. Fill a weekly pill organizer each Sunday.
  3. Wake up at the same time every day.
  4. Keep the bedroom dark and quiet.
  5. Weigh yourself regularly.
  6. Check blood pressure with an upper-arm cuff.
  7. Walk, even for two minutes, five days a week.
  8. Avoid cannabis and stimulants; limit alcohol.
  9. Post the day's plan on a wall calendar.
  10. Keep a notebook of voices and what helped.

For family and friends

  • Share one goal: keeping them out of the hospital.
  • Do not push the diagnosis. Start with common ground: sleep, stress, fear, concentration.
  • Ask whether a symptom bothers them, not whether you still see it.
  • Let them stay the main decision-maker.
  • NAMI Family-to-Family is a free eight-session course for families (nami.org).

Get help right away if

  • Several nights of little or no sleep, or voices that have become commanding or threatening
  • Stopping medication, or missing it for more than a few days
  • High fever with stiff muscles and confusion on an antipsychotic (go to the emergency room)
  • On clozapine: fever, sore throat, or flu-like illness (a same-day blood count is needed)
  • Thoughts of hurting yourself or not wanting to be here: call or text 988 any time, or go to the nearest emergency room. If you are in immediate danger, call 911.

Good to know

  • Report any new repetitive movement of the mouth, tongue, or fingers at once; catching it early gives the best chance of improvement, and effective treatments are available.
  • Book: Surviving Schizophrenia by E. Fuller Torrey, the one to buy if you buy one thing. For quitting smoking: Allen Carr's Easy Way to Stop Smoking, paired with nicotine replacement and your prescriber.
