Schizophrenia

For patients and families. This page explains what schizophrenia involves, what treatment aims to do, and how families can help.

Prepared by Alice Tran, PMHNP-BC, FNP-BC · Alice Tran Psychiatric Care · Updated October 2026

Read the full guide All patient resources

What it is

Schizophrenia is a cluster of symptoms: perceptions others do not share, such as hearing voices; fixed beliefs others do not hold; disorganized thinking; loss of motivation; and difficulty with concentration and memory. Together they interfere with work, study, and relationships, and treatment aims to recover those abilities, not merely to silence the voices. One way to picture the illness is a brain that flags ordinary events as deeply significant and then constructs an explanation for why.

On insight and hope

Limited awareness of the illness is a feature of the illness itself, not obstinacy. Treatment does not depend on the person agreeing with the diagnosis. Someone who says "I do not think I have this, but I take my medication every day" generally does better than someone who accepts the label and stops. Realistic hope is warranted: medication works well, even when it is unpleasant.

Treatment has three parts

  • Medication: the main thing that keeps people out of the hospital. Report sedation, restlessness, or weight gain early, because each can be managed. Ask whether a long-acting injection would suit you, and if several medications have not worked well, ask about clozapine.
  • Body and routine: consistent sleep, a fixed wake time, regular movement, and physical health checks protect both the mind and the heart.
  • Support: family education, practical coping tools such as headphones and music, and a notebook recording voices and what eased them.

Staying on medication

Taking medication consistently is the single most powerful thing within your control.

  • Fill a weekly pill organizer every Sunday, with help if possible.
  • Use an alarm, or a dispenser that notifies family of a missed dose.
  • Ask about a once-nightly schedule.
  • Ask about a monthly or six-monthly injection.

Protecting the body

Lost sleep and relapses tend to arrive together. Alcohol and cannabis both fragment sleep, and in people with this vulnerability cannabis or methamphetamine can tip a brief psychosis into a lasting one. Heart health matters because antipsychotics can raise weight, blood sugar, and cholesterol: weigh yourself regularly, check blood pressure, and keep lab appointments. On clozapine, tell us before stopping smoking, since quitting raises the blood level.

Daily habits

Check one or two to start with, not all ten.

  1. Dose at one fixed hour every day.
  2. Fill the weekly organizer every Sunday.
  3. Wake at the same time daily.
  4. Keep the bedroom dark and quiet.
  5. Weigh yourself on a regular schedule.
  6. Measure blood pressure at home with an arm cuff.
  7. Walk five days a week, even for two minutes.
  8. Avoid cannabis and stimulants; keep alcohol minimal.
  9. Post the day's plan on a wall calendar.
  10. Write down which voices came and what eased them.

For family and friends

  • Agree on one shared goal: staying out of the hospital.
  • Do not insist on the diagnosis. Begin with what you both already agree on: poor sleep, stress, feeling afraid, trouble concentrating.
  • Ask whether a symptom is troubling them, rather than whether you still notice it.
  • Let them remain the main decision-maker.
  • NAMI Family-to-Family is a free eight-session course for relatives (nami.org).

Get help right away if

  • Several nights with little or no sleep, or voices that have become commanding or threatening
  • Medication stopped, or missed for more than a few days
  • High fever with stiff muscles and confusion while taking an antipsychotic: go to the emergency room
  • On clozapine: fever, sore throat, or flu-like illness requires a same-day blood count
  • On clozapine: severe constipation or no bowel movement for three days, or chest pain, breathlessness, or fever in the first months, needs same-day attention
  • Thoughts of hurting yourself or not wanting to be here: call or text 988 any time, or go to the nearest emergency room. If you are in immediate danger, call 911.

Good to know

  • Report any new repetitive movement of the mouth, tongue, or fingers promptly. Tardive dyskinesia rarely reverses on its own, but adjusting the medication early limits it, and VMAT2 inhibitor medications can treat it.
  • Book: Surviving Schizophrenia by E. Fuller Torrey, the most useful single purchase. For stopping smoking, varenicline with nicotine replacement works well; ask your prescriber, and tell us first if you take clozapine.
