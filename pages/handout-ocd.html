OCD

You have been diagnosed with OCD. This page explains what that means, how it is treated, and what you can do now.

Prepared by Alice Tran, PMHNP-BC, FNP-BC · Alice Tran Psychiatric Care · Updated October 2026

Read the full guide All patient resources

What it is

Obsessive-compulsive disorder is a cycle of obsessions, unwanted intrusive thoughts, images, or urges that cause anxiety, and compulsions, the mental or physical rituals done to relieve that anxiety. The relief is brief, and the ritual teaches the brain the thought was dangerous, so it returns. OCD is not about being neat. It is a treatable anxiety-driven loop.

Common signs

  • Intrusive thoughts about contamination, harm, mistakes, morality, relationships, or sexuality that feel alien and distressing
  • Checking, washing, counting, arranging, or repeating until it feels right
  • Mental rituals: reviewing, praying, neutralizing, seeking certainty
  • Asking for reassurance, confessing, or avoiding triggers
  • Knowing the fear is excessive and being unable to stop anyway
  • Hours a day lost to the cycle

Why it happens

OCD runs in families and involves overactivity in brain circuits that flag errors and danger. Everyone has intrusive thoughts; in OCD the brain treats them as meaningful and urgent, and the compulsions, by bringing relief, lock the pattern in. Having a disturbing thought says nothing about your character. Reacting to it is what the disorder is.

Why it keeps going

OCD is a loop with a fixed order: a trigger, an unwanted thought, a frightening interpretation of what the thought means, distress, and a ritual that relieves it. The thought itself is ordinary; surveys find that about nine in ten people without OCD report the same sort of intrusions. The interpretation is the problem. Because the ritual brings relief, the brain concludes the ritual was necessary and the loop strengthens. Rituals are often invisible: mentally reviewing, checking one's own reactions, praying a thought away.

OCD is not a defect in reasoning, and the content of the thought matters far less than the response to it. It is not ordinary worry, and it says nothing about character: an unwanted thought is neither a wish nor a plan. The intrusions aim at whatever the person values most, which is why devoted parents get images of harming their children and religious people get blasphemous ones. Techniques for calming or arguing the thought away, used to make it leave, feed the loop rather than breaking it.

Treatment has three parts

  • Exposure and response prevention: The first-line treatment. Triggers are approached in planned steps while the ritual is withheld. It is slow and uncomfortable, and it is effective, including for purely mental obsessions.
  • Medication: SSRIs, usually at higher doses than for depression, over eight to twelve weeks. Expect meaningful relief rather than a cure. Combined with ERP, results are better than either alone.
  • Daily practice: Withdraw reassurance, checking, and online searching. Regular aerobic exercise helps. OCD is managed rather than cured, and flare-ups under stress are expected.

How it is treated

  • Exposure and response prevention (ERP) is the first-line therapy: facing the trigger on purpose and not doing the ritual, in gradual steps, until the brain learns the fear is false. A referral to an ERP-trained therapist can be arranged.
  • Medication: SSRIs are first-line, often at higher doses and for longer than in depression: eight to twelve weeks for full effect. Clomipramine is an alternative. Combining medication with ERP works best for moderate to severe OCD.
  • Supportive therapy is part of every visit here.
  • Follow-up: every two to four weeks while the dose is adjusted, then monthly, then every three months once stable.

In the moment

  • Label it: "That is the OCD."
  • Refuse to settle the question: "Maybe, maybe not. I am not spending more of today on it."
  • Allow the discomfort and carry on with the task in front of you. Stay in the room; finish what you started.
  • Do not debate, analyze, or test the thought. Whatever it demands, do the reverse.
  • For family: reassurance is a ritual performed on the person's behalf. An agreed response helps: "That sounds like the OCD asking. I love you, and I will not answer it."

Habits that help

Pick one or two to begin with; the full list is a menu, not an assignment.

  1. Withhold the ritual, including mental ones.
  2. Do the reverse of what the thought demands.
  3. List your triggers and score each from 0 to 100.
  4. Start in the middle of the list and work upward.
  5. Write out the worst-case scenario and read it aloud daily until it loses its charge.
  6. Postpone the ritual a little longer each time.
  7. Stop searching, checking, confessing, and self-testing.
  8. Reduce avoidance one step at a time.
  9. Exercise aerobically on most days.
  10. Say the thoughts aloud to your therapist, however ugly they sound.

Rule out look-alikes

  • Generalized worry, which circles real-life problems rather than intrusive images
  • Health anxiety, body dysmorphic disorder, tics, or hoarding, which call for related but distinct plans
  • Psychosis is different: recognizing the thoughts as your own and finding them repugnant are signs of intact reality testing
  • Suicidal thoughts accompanied by a wish, a plan, or relief are not OCD and need immediate help: call or text 988

Medication, briefly

SSRIs are the first choice, typically at higher doses and for longer than in depression, with eight to twelve weeks needed for full effect. Clomipramine is an alternative. When ERP and medication have not been enough, TMS, a form of brain stimulation, can be discussed. Seek out a therapist whose practice centers on ERP for OCD; the International OCD Foundation directory at iocdf.org is the place to look. Real ERP has three features: a ranked list of triggers, exposures scheduled in advance, and a firm rule against reassurance. Exploratory talk therapy about what the thoughts mean can worsen OCD.

Get help right away if

  • Thoughts of hurting yourself, including from exhaustion with the cycle
  • Being unable to eat, leave the house, or care for yourself because of rituals
  • New or worsening thoughts of self-harm in the first weeks of a new medication
  • Thoughts of hurting yourself or not wanting to be here: call or text 988 any time, or go to the nearest emergency room

Good to know

  • Harm-related and taboo intrusive thoughts are among the most common OCD themes, and having them does not mean you want to act on them.
  • Improvement from medication is slow; judging it before ten to twelve weeks is the most common mistake.
  • Family members can learn to support treatment without participating in rituals. Ask us for guidance.
  • Books: Freedom from Obsessive Compulsive Disorder by Jonathan Grayson; The OCD Workbook by Hyman and Pedrick, whose chapter on mental rituals is especially useful. International OCD Foundation: iocdf.org. The intrusive thoughts handout on this site goes deeper into the purely mental form.
