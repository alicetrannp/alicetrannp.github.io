Getting Care

How a psychiatric provider treats anxiety
differently from primary care

Written & medically reviewed by Alice Tran, PMHNP-BC  ·  September 2026  ·  7 min read

Your primary care doctor is often the first and best place to start when anxiety becomes a problem. They screen for it, they start effective medications every day, and for many people that is all that is ever needed. This article is not about who is better. It is about what changes when anxiety is complicated, stubborn, or not responding the way you hoped, and why a psychiatric provider's visit is often built differently for exactly those situations.

If you have wondered whether seeing a specialist would actually change anything, here is a concrete look at four things a psychiatric visit tends to do differently: time, dose adjustment, medication combinations, and follow-up cadence.

The short answer

A psychiatric visit for anxiety is built around four things: more time to pin down the specific diagnosis, deliberate dose titration to a genuinely therapeutic level, medication combinations and switches when a first agent only partly works, and closer follow-up early on. It adds to your primary care, it does not replace it.

More time to sort out what is actually happening

Anxiety rarely travels alone. It overlaps with depression, ADHD, trauma, sleep problems, thyroid issues, and substance use, and the outward symptoms of all of these can look nearly identical. A psychiatric evaluation is built to spend the time untangling this: a detailed clinical interview, symptom rating scales, a review of your full psychiatric and medical history, and careful attention to which specific anxiety disorder you have. Generalized anxiety, social anxiety, and panic disorder are treated somewhat differently.

This matters because studies in primary care show anxiety is frequently either missed or labeled with a nonspecific "anxiety" diagnosis, and getting the specific diagnosis right is what makes evidence-based treatment work. A longer, structured first visit is where that precision comes from. At this practice the initial evaluation is up to 60 minutes.

Dose adjustment: finding the right dose, not just a dose

The most common reason a good medication does not work is that it was never optimized. Anxiety guidelines recommend starting antidepressants low, because people with anxiety are unusually sensitive to early side effects like jitteriness, and then deliberately titrating upward to a genuinely therapeutic dose over time. Anxiolytic effects typically begin around 2 to 4 weeks, and improvement can keep building over weeks to months.

A psychiatric provider's visits are structured around this titration:

  • What dose you are on and how you tolerated the last increase
  • Whether you have reached an adequate dose, and how long you have been there
  • Whether the trial has been a fair test before concluding a medication has failed

Medication combinations and advanced strategies

When a first medication is only partially effective, a brief visit is rarely the right setting for what comes next. Psychiatric care routinely goes further:

  • Switching to a different first-line agent
  • Combining medications thoughtfully, with attention to interactions and side effects
  • Adding a short-acting, as-needed option while a daily medication takes effect
  • Addressing a co-existing condition, such as ADHD or depression, that is feeding the anxiety

Because these strategies carry more nuance, they are the everyday work of a specialist rather than an occasional add-on.

Follow-up cadence: closer monitoring, especially early on

Perhaps the biggest practical difference is how often you are seen. Evidence consistently shows that close, systematic follow-up, checking symptoms with the same rating scale each visit and adjusting promptly, is one of the strongest predictors of actually getting better. In the early phase of treatment or during dose changes, that often means visits every few weeks rather than every few months, then spacing out as you stabilize. The goal is not more appointments for their own sake. It is catching a stalled response or a fixable side effect before it derails your progress.

This is a partnership with your primary care doctor

Seeing a psychiatric provider does not mean leaving your family doctor. The best-studied model, collaborative care, has primary care clinicians, a care manager, and a consulting psychiatric specialist all working together, and it outperforms usual care. Think of specialty psychiatric care as adding depth to the anxiety part of your care while your primary care doctor continues to manage the rest of your health. With your permission, visit summaries can be shared with your primary care office.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should I see my primary care doctor or a psychiatric provider for anxiety?

Primary care is often the right first stop, and for many people it is all that is needed. A psychiatric provider is worth considering when anxiety is complicated by other conditions, when a first medication has not worked after a fair trial, or when you want visits built specifically around getting the diagnosis and the medication right.

How often will I be seen for anxiety at a psychiatric practice?

Early in treatment and during dose changes, usually every few weeks rather than every few months, then spacing out as you stabilize. Close, systematic follow-up is one of the strongest predictors of actually getting better.

Do I have to stop seeing my primary care doctor?

No. Specialty psychiatric care adds depth to the anxiety part of your care while your primary care doctor continues to manage the rest of your health. The best-studied model, collaborative care, has both working together.

How long is a first anxiety evaluation at Alice Tran Psychiatric Care?

Up to 60 minutes, in person in Fairfax or by secure video anywhere in Virginia. Follow-up visits are 30 minutes. Records from your primary care provider are welcome.

If your anxiety has been treated but never quite resolved, or you want a provider whose entire visit is built around getting the diagnosis and the medication right, support is available.
