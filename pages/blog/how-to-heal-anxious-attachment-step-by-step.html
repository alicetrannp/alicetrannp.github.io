I Have an Anxious Attachment Style. How Do I Fix It? (And What If I Am Actually Avoidant?)

Written & medically reviewed by Alice Tran, PMHNP-BC  ·  August 2026  ·  11 min read

First, the most important and most hopeful thing: attachment styles can change. They feel permanent because they were laid down early and run automatically, but they are not your fixed destiny. Research consistently shows that with new, reliably safe relationship experiences, and with therapy understood as a corrective attachment experience, insecure people can move toward security. Clinicians even have a name for this: earned security.

So if you have read that you are anxiously attached and felt both relieved and doomed, let go of the second part. This is workable.

Before you start: are you sure you are anxious?

Most people who research attachment assume they are the anxious one, because anxious attachment is loud. It protests, pursues, and reaches for reassurance in ways that are easy to notice. But it is genuinely common to mismatch your own style, so it is worth an honest check before building a plan around the wrong label.

  • Anxious (preoccupied) tends to look like craving closeness, fearing abandonment, needing reassurance, overthinking texts, feeling panicky at distance, protesting when a partner pulls away.
  • Avoidant (dismissing) tends to look like valuing independence above all, feeling suffocated when someone gets close, going quiet under stress, minimizing your own needs, pulling back exactly when things get serious.
  • Fearful-avoidant (disorganized) is the confusing overlap: you crave closeness and fear it, so you reach out and then retreat, hot then cold, sometimes with the same person.

Here is the catch that trips people up. Some people pursue when anxious but shut down entirely when truly overwhelmed. And plenty of people are anxious with an avoidant partner but calmer with a secure one, because attachment shows up in relationship, not in a vacuum. Do not over-identify with one box.

Step 1: learn your own pattern in real time

You cannot change what you cannot see. The first job is to notice your attachment system firing: the surge of panic when a text goes unanswered, the urge to pull away when someone gets close. Name it as it happens: this is my attachment system activating, not necessarily reality. This present-moment noticing, distinguishing what is happening now from old wounds, is exactly the skill research links to reductions in anxious attachment.

Step 2: build self-regulation, not just reassurance

For anxious attachment, the core skill is learning to soothe your own nervous system so one person does not have to be your only off switch. This is not about needing less. It is about being able to steady yourself while you wait, so you can reach out from calm instead of panic. Mindfulness and loving-kindness meditation have been shown to increase positive emotion and decrease negative emotion specifically for people higher in attachment anxiety, sometimes with a dose-response effect.

Step 3: communicate needs directly, then let reassurance land

Anxious attachment often expresses needs indirectly: testing, hinting, protesting, or seeking reassurance in a loop that never fully satisfies. The secure alternative is a clear, single, direct request. The harder half is then actually taking the reassurance in rather than immediately doubting it. Practicing receiving is as important as practicing asking.

Step 4: choose and notice safe, consistent people

Attachment change happens through corrective experiences, repeated evidence that closeness is safe and reliable. Frequent small positive relationship experiences, and behaviors that clearly validate the relationship, have been shown to move people toward security over time. This is also why it matters to notice when someone’s inconsistency keeps your alarm permanently on. If you tend to pick people who cannot quite show up, that pattern has its own explanation.

Step 5: rewrite the self-worth story

Underneath anxious attachment is often a quiet belief: I have to earn love, and it could vanish. Part of the work is building worth that does not depend on a partner’s moment-to-moment availability, through friendships, meaning, competence, and self-compassion. Self-compassion in particular is a well-supported mechanism of change in attachment-based therapies.

Step 6: get support that fits your style

The strongest evidence for lasting change is in therapy. Attachment-based couples therapy, meaning Emotionally Focused Therapy, reliably reduces both attachment anxiety and avoidance and helps partners build a secure bond, with benefits holding at follow-up. Individual attachment-focused therapy also works, and research suggests people low in attachment security do better in therapies that include an explicit interpersonal focus rather than purely technical ones.

But what if I am actually avoidant?

If Step 4 made you think honestly, closeness is what feels suffocating, not distance, you may lean avoidant, and the plan shifts in important ways. Avoidant attachment is not the absence of needs. It is needs that got buried early because expressing them did not work, so independence became armor. The good news is that it is just as changeable. But the approach is almost the mirror image of the anxious one.

  • Notice the shutdown, not the spike. Where anxious people feel a surge, avoidant people often feel a click-off, going numb, busy, or distant right when things get emotionally real. The skill is catching that automatic withdrawal before you act on it.
  • Beware the chase-and-dodge trap. Avoidant people heal with space, not pressure. Being pushed to open up tends to trigger more retreat. Growth comes from choosing, in small doses, to stay and share slightly more than feels comfortable, on your own terms rather than under demand.
  • Take affection and support in small, then larger, doses. Research found something striking: low or moderate support can feel like control or criticism and push avoidant people away, but high, unambiguous support and clearly tailored affection actually get through, offering undeniable evidence that the other person is safely available.
  • Practice naming one feeling or need at a time. Avoidant attachment minimizes needs. Recovery is relearning that having and voicing a need will not lead to engulfment or rejection.
  • Therapy still works, with the right pacing. For avoidant clients, effective therapists stay engaged but do not crowd. Attachment-based couples therapy has been shown to lower attachment avoidance specifically, and reductions in avoidance are among the strongest predictors of lasting relationship satisfaction.

And if you are both (fearful-avoidant)?

Fearful-avoidant means anxious and avoidant at once. Use the anxious tools when you are pursuing and panicking, and the avoidant tools when you are shutting down and fleeing. This pattern benefits most from working with a skilled therapist.

Many people who feel confused reading this are fearful-avoidant, anxious and avoidant at once. The steps still apply. You will use the anxious tools when you are pursuing and panicking, and the avoidant tools when you are shutting down and fleeing. The unifying goal is the same: teaching your nervous system, through repeated safe experience, that closeness is not dangerous and distance is not abandonment. This pattern often has roots in more complex early experiences, and it is the one where working with a skilled therapist tends to matter most.

What fixed actually looks like

You are not aiming to never feel a pang of insecurity. Secure people feel those too. You are aiming for something quieter: being able to feel the old alarm without letting it drive. To ask for what you need without testing. To let someone close without bracing for loss, or to hold onto yourself without pushing love away. That is earned security, and the fact that you are asking how to fix this means the work has already begun.
