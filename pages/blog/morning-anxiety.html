Anxiety

Waking Up With Dread
why anxiety is worse in the morning (and what helps)

Written & medically reviewed by Alice Tran, PMHNP-BC  ·  August 2026  ·  7 min read

Your alarm goes off. Before you've even opened your eyes fully, it hits: a wave of dread, a tight stomach, a racing mind already cataloging everything that could go wrong today. You haven't done anything yet, and you already feel overwhelmed.

If anxiety is the worst thing you feel each morning, you're experiencing something so common it has a name in everyday language: morning anxiety. And there's a biological reason it happens.

Cortisol: Your Body's Built In Alarm Clock

Your body runs on an internal clock, and one of its most reliable features is something scientists call the cortisol awakening response, or CAR. Every morning, about 20 to 45 minutes after you wake up, your adrenal glands release a surge of cortisol, the body's primary stress hormone.

This isn't a malfunction. In a well regulated system, the cortisol awakening response is designed to help you wake up alert, focused, and ready to face the day. Cortisol mobilizes energy, sharpens attention, and prepares your brain and body for action. After that morning peak, cortisol gradually declines throughout the day, reaching its lowest levels around midnight.

Why Anxiety Hijacks the Morning Surge

Here's where it gets interesting. Research has found that people with anxiety disorders, particularly persistent anxiety, tend to have a steeper and more exaggerated cortisol awakening response. In other words, their bodies produce a bigger spike of cortisol upon waking than people without anxiety.

Studies in both adolescents and adults have confirmed this pattern. One study of nearly 1,800 young people found that those with persistent anxiety problems had significantly higher morning cortisol levels and a more pronounced cortisol awakening response. Another study found that anxious patients showed a steeper 30 minute cortisol increase after waking, along with a more dynamic pattern of cortisol reactivity throughout the day.

So when you wake up already feeling dread, it's not weakness or a character flaw. It's your stress response system overshooting its mark.

The Role of Sleep (or Lack of It)

Sleep and anxiety have a complicated relationship. Anxiety makes it harder to fall asleep and stay asleep. Poor sleep, in turn, makes your nervous system more reactive the next day, which amplifies anxiety. It's a vicious cycle.

When you haven't slept well, your prefrontal cortex (the rational, planning part of your brain) is less effective at keeping the amygdala (your emotional alarm system) in check. That means your brain is more likely to interpret neutral situations as threatening. Add an exaggerated cortisol surge on top of that, and you have a recipe for waking up in a state of alarm.

Why Your Mind Races Before Your Feet Hit the Floor

There's a psychological component too. During sleep, your brain isn't processing and organizing worries the way it does during waking hours. When you wake up, your conscious mind comes back online and is immediately flooded with all the concerns, responsibilities, and "what ifs" that were on pause. For people with generalized anxiety disorder, this can feel like walking into a room where someone left every faucet running.

What Actually Helps

You can't eliminate the cortisol awakening response, and you wouldn't want to. But you can change your relationship with those first minutes of the day.

Don't reach for your phone immediately. Emails, news, and social media give your already activated brain a stream of new stimuli to worry about. Give yourself 15 to 20 minutes before engaging with the outside world.

Use your breath to shift your nervous system. Slow, deep breathing with a longer exhale activates your parasympathetic nervous system and helps counterbalance the cortisol surge. Even five minutes of breathing practice (in for 4 seconds, out for 6 to 8 seconds) can measurably lower heart rate and anxiety.

Move early. Exercise doesn't just distract you from anxiety. Evidence shows that physical activity has a genuine anti anxiety effect. A morning walk, some stretching, or a short workout helps metabolize excess cortisol and adrenaline and resets your nervous system for the day.

Eat something. Skipping breakfast can worsen the physical symptoms of anxiety. Low blood sugar mimics and amplifies the feeling of anxiety: shakiness, lightheadedness, irritability. A small, balanced breakfast helps stabilize your blood sugar and gives your brain the fuel it needs.

Prepare the night before. Much of morning anxiety comes from anticipatory dread about what lies ahead. Laying out your clothes, packing your bag, or writing a short to do list before bed can reduce the number of decisions your already overwhelmed brain has to make first thing.

Consider professional support. Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) is the most effective psychological treatment for anxiety disorders, with benefits that last long term. CBT can help you identify and restructure the catastrophic thinking patterns that tend to flood in each morning. Medications such as SSRIs and SNRIs can also reduce overall anxiety levels, which helps flatten that exaggerated cortisol spike over time.

The Bottom Line

Morning anxiety isn't a sign that the day is going to be terrible. It's a sign that your stress response system is reacting too strongly to the simple act of waking up. Understanding the biology behind it, the cortisol spike, the sleep connection, the racing thoughts, can help take some of the fear out of those difficult first minutes. With the right strategies and support, mornings can get easier.
