Why Does Anxiety Cause Physical Symptoms?
chest tightness, racing heart, and what your body is doing

Written & medically reviewed by Alice Tran, PMHNP-BC  ·  August 2026  ·  7 min read

You feel your heart hammering against your ribs. Your chest gets tight. Your breathing feels shallow and strange. And the first thought that races through your mind isn't "I'm anxious." It's "Something is seriously wrong with my heart."

You're not alone. Millions of people end up in emergency rooms every year convinced they're having a heart attack, only to learn that what they experienced was anxiety. That doesn't make it any less real. The physical symptoms of anxiety are genuine, measurable, and sometimes overwhelming. Understanding why your body does this can be the first step toward taking back control.

Your Brain Thinks You're in Danger (Even When You're Not)

Deep inside your brain, a small almond shaped structure called the amygdala acts as your alarm system. Its job is to detect threats and launch a survival response before your conscious mind even catches up. When the amygdala fires, it triggers a cascade through your sympathetic nervous system, the body's "fight or flight" wiring.

Here's what happens in seconds: your adrenal glands flood your bloodstream with adrenaline (epinephrine) and norepinephrine. These stress hormones are designed to prepare you to fight a predator or run from danger. The problem? Your brain can't always tell the difference between a real threat and an anxious thought about tomorrow's meeting.

Why Your Heart Races and Your Chest Gets Tight

Adrenaline binds to receptors on your heart, directly increasing your heart rate and the force of each contraction. Your blood pressure rises. Blood is redirected away from your digestive system and toward your muscles so you can run or fight. Your breathing quickens to take in more oxygen.

That chest tightness? It comes from a combination of factors: your chest wall muscles tense up, your breathing pattern shifts to rapid, shallow breaths, and the increased heart rate creates a pounding sensation that can feel like pressure or pain. Your intercostal muscles (the muscles between your ribs) can cramp from overuse during rapid breathing, adding to the sensation that something is wrong.

The Full List of What Anxiety Can Do to Your Body

The sympathetic nervous system touches nearly every organ in your body. That's why anxiety doesn't just affect your chest and heart. It can cause:

  • Sweating, even when you're not hot
  • Trembling or shaking
  • Nausea, stomach cramps, or diarrhea (your digestive system shuts down during fight or flight)
  • Dizziness or lightheadedness (from changes in blood pressure and breathing patterns)
  • Tingling or numbness in your hands and feet (from hyperventilation, which shifts your blood's pH)
  • Feeling of choking or a lump in your throat
  • Hot flashes or chills
  • Muscle tension, especially in your neck, jaw, and shoulders

These aren't imaginary symptoms. They are the predictable, measurable result of your nervous system doing exactly what it was designed to do.

So How Do You Know It's Anxiety and Not Your Heart?

This is one of the most important questions people ask, and it deserves a straight answer: if you're experiencing chest pain, a racing heart, or shortness of breath for the first time, or if your symptoms feel different from what you've experienced before, get checked out by a doctor. Anxiety and cardiac conditions can produce overlapping symptoms. A thorough history, physical exam, and sometimes an electrocardiogram (ECG) can help distinguish between the two.

That said, there are some patterns that can be helpful to know. Anxiety related chest discomfort tends to be sharp or stabbing, often localized to one spot, and frequently accompanied by a sense of dread, tingling, or hyperventilation. It usually peaks within minutes and then gradually fades. Cardiac chest pain is more often described as pressure or squeezing, may radiate to the arm, jaw, or back, and tends to worsen with physical exertion.

But never self diagnose. Always let a healthcare professional help you sort it out.

What You Can Do Right Now

Once you understand that your body is running a survival program that doesn't match your actual situation, you can start to interrupt the cycle.

Slow your breathing. When you breathe slowly and deeply, especially with a longer exhale than inhale, you activate your parasympathetic nervous system, the "rest and digest" counterpart to fight or flight. Try breathing in for 4 seconds and out for 6 to 8 seconds. Research shows that structured abdominal breathing can measurably reduce heart rate, blood pressure, and anxiety scores.

Name what's happening. Simply saying to yourself "This is adrenaline. My body thinks I'm in danger, but I'm safe" can reduce the fear of the symptoms themselves, which is often what keeps the cycle going.

Move your body. Adrenaline is designed to fuel physical activity. A brisk walk, some jumping jacks, or even shaking out your hands can help metabolize the stress hormones circulating in your system.

Get professional support. Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) is the most well studied psychological treatment for anxiety disorders, with strong evidence across generalized anxiety disorder, social anxiety, and panic disorder. CBT helps you identify the thought patterns that trigger your alarm system and teaches you concrete skills to respond differently. If therapy isn't enough on its own, medications like SSRIs and SNRIs are effective first line options that your doctor can discuss with you.

The Bottom Line

Your racing heart and tight chest are not signs that your body is broken. They're signs that your body's protection system is working overtime. The good news is that once you understand the mechanism, you can learn to work with your nervous system instead of against it. Talk to your doctor, explore therapy, and remember: the symptoms are real, but the danger usually isn't.
