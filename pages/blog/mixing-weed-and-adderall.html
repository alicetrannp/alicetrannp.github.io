ADHD & Cannabis

Mixing Weed and Adderall:
The Heart Risks Nobody Mentions

Written & medically reviewed by Alice Tran, PMHNP-BC  ·  August 2026  ·  9 min read

If you take a stimulant for ADHD and also use cannabis, whether to unwind, to sleep, or because a friend said it balances out the Adderall jitters, you are far from alone. Cannabis use is more common in adults with ADHD than in the general population, and many people quietly combine the two without ever mentioning it to their prescriber.

The part most people never hear

Your ADHD stimulant and THC both speed up your heart and raise your blood pressure. When you use them together, those effects add on top of each other. Two substances pushing your cardiovascular system in the same direction, at the same time, is a genuine medical concern, especially in young adults who assume they are too healthy to worry about their heart.

This is not about morality or legality. This post explains what actually happens in your body when you mix cannabis with a stimulant, why it matters even if you feel fine, and what to talk through with your prescriber.

Why Stimulants Alone Already Raise Your Heart Rate and Blood Pressure

ADHD stimulants work by boosting dopamine and norepinephrine, and norepinephrine is the same fight-or-flight signal that revs up your cardiovascular system. This is a known, expected effect of the medication, not a malfunction.

A meta-analysis of ADHD medication trials found that stimulants raise resting heart rate by about 5.7 beats per minute and systolic blood pressure by about 2.0 mm Hg on average. For most healthy people this is well tolerated and monitored by a prescriber. But it means your baseline on medication is already running slightly hot, and that becomes the starting point when you add anything else that stresses the heart.

THC Does the Same Thing, Through a Different Door

THC, the psychoactive part of cannabis, also activates your sympathetic nervous system while suppressing the rest-and-digest side.

What the American Heart Association describes

THC increases heart rate, myocardial oxygen demand, and blood pressure, while also promoting platelet activation and endothelial dysfunction, meaning damage to the lining of your blood vessels.

In the short term, cannabis can trigger a racing heart, premature beats, and even atrial fibrillation. Edibles, because they hit harder and later than expected, are especially likely to send people to the emergency room with cardiac and psychiatric symptoms. Importantly, this is mostly a THC effect. CBD alone tends to have the opposite, milder profile and does not drive heart rate up the way THC does.

The Real Issue: The Effects Stack On Top of Each Other

This is the part patients rarely realize. A controlled human study specifically tested methylphenidate, the active ingredient in Ritalin and Concerta, combined with THC.

The two effects are additive

The study found additive effects on heart rate and on rate-pressure product, a measure of how hard your heart is working. Peak heart rate climbed stepwise as the stimulant dose increased on top of THC, reaching over 100 beats per minute in the combined condition.

In plain terms: the medication nudges your heart up, THC nudges it up further, and together they push harder than either does alone. For a young, healthy person this may just feel like an uncomfortable racing heart or anxiety. But it also means more work for the heart and more oxygen demand at the same moment, exactly the kind of strain that matters if there is any hidden cardiovascular vulnerability.

Why “I Am Young and Healthy” Is Not a Free Pass

The most sobering cannabis data comes from young, otherwise healthy people.

A roughly six-fold higher risk of heart attack

A large 2025 analysis in JAMA of more than 4.6 million adults aged 50 or younger, with no prior heart disease and no tobacco use, found cannabis use associated with a roughly six-fold higher risk of heart attack compared with non-users.

It is not only heart attacks

Cannabis has also been linked to stroke, arrhythmias, and cardiovascular death across multiple studies, with proposed mechanisms including coronary vasospasm and endothelial dysfunction.

A 2026 review went further, describing cannabis as a potentially modifiable risk factor for heart attack specifically in younger adults without traditional risk factors, the exact group most likely to assume they are immune. Layering a stimulant’s cardiovascular effects on top of this is not a combination the evidence supports treating casually.

There Are Also Drug-Level Interactions

Beyond the heart, cannabis can alter how your ADHD medication is processed. Both THC and CBD inhibit CES1, the enzyme that breaks down methylphenidate, and pharmacokinetic modeling predicts this can modestly raise methylphenidate blood levels. CBD may increase exposure by up to roughly 55 percent under certain repeated-dosing scenarios. Higher drug levels can mean more side effects, including more cardiovascular stimulation. Cannabis also broadly affects the liver’s CYP450 enzyme system, which is why it interacts with a long list of medications.

On top of that, regular cannabis use makes ADHD itself harder to treat. It clouds the assessment of whether your medication is actually working, and daily users with ADHD frequently report worse inattention and more stimulant side effects.

What This Means For You: Talk to Your Prescriber, Honestly

None of this means you are in trouble for using cannabis. It means your prescriber needs the full picture to keep you safe. Being honest about cannabis lets your provider:

  • Accurately monitor your heart rate and blood pressure on stimulant therapy
  • Recognize palpitations, chest tightness, or panic as possible warning signs. These tend to appear within the first one to two hours after cannabis use.
  • Adjust your ADHD treatment plan so it is actually working, rather than being masked by cannabis
  • Screen for any personal or family cardiac history that raises the stakes of combining the two

When to seek medical attention, not wait it out

If you notice a racing or pounding heartbeat, chest pain, shortness of breath, fainting, or severe anxiety after using cannabis, especially while on a stimulant, treat it as a reason to seek medical attention. Call 911 for chest pain, fainting, or trouble breathing.
