Anxiety as a habit:
how worry keeps it going

Written & medically reviewed by Alice Tran, PMHNP-BC  ·  October 2026  ·  7 min read

In the office I often ask a patient to separate two words they have been using interchangeably. One names a sensation. The other names an activity. Keeping them apart turns out to be the first step in interrupting a pattern that can run for years.

The short answer

The sensation of unease is anxiety. The mental activity of rehearsing bad outcomes is worry. Worry delivers a brief sense of control, the brain registers that as a reward, and the loop repeats: unease, rehearsal, relief, unease. Breaking it does not require certainty about the future. It requires noticing the loop, checking whether any real action exists, doing that action or deliberately leaving the question open, and returning attention to the present. Repetition does the rest.

A sensation and an activity

  • Anxiety is bodily and immediate: a knot below the ribs, a restless urgency, a sense that something must be handled now. It arrives uninvited.
  • Worry is mental and voluntary, even when it does not feel that way: running scenarios, cataloguing what could go wrong, drafting responses to events that have not happened. It is almost always aimed at the future.

Because worry is something a person does rather than something that happens to them, it can be practiced differently. The sensation will still come. The response to it is trainable.

The habit loop behind worry

Psychiatrist Judson Brewer, who studies habit formation, frames chronic worry the way behavioral science frames any habit: a cue, a behavior, and a reward. The framing extends a 1985 proposal by psychologist Thomas Borkovec that worry is not merely a symptom of anxiety but one of its maintaining causes.

  1. Cue: a flicker of unease, an open question, or a thought about a possible problem.
  2. Behavior: mental rehearsal. Scenarios, contingencies, imagined conversations.
  3. Reward: a momentary sense of having done something about the problem.

The reward is genuine but short-lived, because rehearsal does not resolve uncertainty. Holding a threatening scenario in mind keeps the threat response active, the brief relief evaporates, the unease returns, and the next cue is already in place. Each cycle strengthens the association between uncertainty and worrying.

Picture an accountant with a performance review on Friday. On Wednesday evening a small dread surfaces, and he begins drafting answers to criticisms that have not been made. For a short while this passes for preparation. By late evening he is more agitated than when he started, nothing about Friday has changed, and the dread has been reinforced rather than discharged.

Why the activity feels productive

Unease carries an impulse toward action. When a problem is present and concrete, acting on the impulse is correct. When the problem lies in the future, there is no action available tonight, and the impulse needs somewhere to go. Mental rehearsal absorbs it. Having a task feels better than sitting with an open question, which is why the loop is so easy to enter and so hard to notice from inside. The gap between feeling better for a moment and having solved anything is the engine of the habit.

A four-step response

Complete certainty about outcomes is not available to anyone, and additional thinking does not produce it. What can be practiced is tolerating an open question without answering it with rehearsal.

  1. Label it. Silently or aloud: "That is unease, and the worrying is starting." Naming the pattern creates a small separation between you and it.
  2. Examine it. What cued this? What am I doing in response? Does this rehearsal move any real problem forward today?
  3. Act, or leave it open. If a concrete step exists, take it now or schedule it. If none exists yet, let the question stay unanswered on purpose. That is uncomfortable and it is the skill.
  4. Return. Notice how the unease sits in the body, with curiosity rather than alarm, and then put attention back on whatever you were doing.

For the accountant on Wednesday evening, the sequence is short. He labels the start of the loop, asks whether any preparation is possible tonight, identifies one real task (jotting two points he wants to raise), completes it, and then lets the rest of the question stay open while he returns to his evening.

The takeaway

The response has to be repeated many times before it feels natural, and that repetition is the treatment. Over weeks, the brain learns a different association: uncertainty can be present without being rehearsed.

When worry needs more than a habit change

The habit model describes many people accurately and is not a complete account for everyone. Anxiety disorders are real conditions with effective treatments, and some worry is driven by depression, trauma, or panic rather than by a habit loop. If worry is consuming your days, disrupting sleep, or impairing work, or if it arrives with panic attacks, nightmares, or a persistent low mood, bring it to an evaluation rather than managing it alone.

A note on medication

When worry is severe, an SSRI can lower its intensity enough to make the four-step response practicable. Benzodiazepines are not prescribed for daily or long-term use at Alice Tran Psychiatric Care, because they blunt the learning the practice depends on.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is all worry a habit?

No. Thinking through a concrete problem once, ending in an action, is planning. Worry is the open-ended version that repeats without resolving. Asking whether a step exists in the next day or two separates the two.

How does this relate to graded practice for anxiety?

Graded practice addresses avoidance of situations and sensations. The habit-loop approach addresses the mental act of worrying. Most people benefit from both; the general anxiety guide on this site covers graded practice, the worry period, and written problem-solving.

How long before the four steps feel natural?

People commonly notice the loop earlier within two or three weeks and feel its pull weaken over a few months. Repetition, not insight, changes the habit.

Can I combine this with therapy or medication?

Yes. The approach sits comfortably inside cognitive behavioral and acceptance-based therapies and alongside an SSRI.

If worry has become the background noise of your days, an evaluation can sort out what is driving it.
