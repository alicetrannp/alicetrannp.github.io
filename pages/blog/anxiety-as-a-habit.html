Anxiety as a habit:
how worry keeps it going

Written & medically reviewed by Alice Tran, PMHNP-BC  ·  October 2026  ·  7 min read

People use "anxiety" and "worry" as if they mean the same thing, and in everyday talk that is fine. For getting out of the loop, it helps to pull them apart. Anxiety is a feeling. Worry is what you do about it. And worrying can turn into a habit that keeps the anxiety coming back.

The short answer

A feeling of uncertainty arrives; you worry, which feels productive and brings a moment of relief; the relief fades and the anxiety returns as the next trigger. Each loop teaches your brain that worry is what to do with uncertainty. The way out is to notice the loop, get curious, act on anything real or allow the not-knowing, and bring attention back to the present. It takes many repetitions, and it works.

Two different things

  • Anxiety, a feeling: unease about something uncertain or still to come. You notice it in the body: a tight chest, a knot in the stomach, restlessness, a sense that something has to be done right now.
  • Worry, something you do: the thinking that follows the feeling. Going over what might happen, picturing the worst, rehearsing how you would handle it. It is almost always about the future.

A feeling arrives on its own. Worrying is a response to it, and responses can change with practice.

The worry loop

Psychiatrist Jud Brewer describes anxiety as a habit loop, the same kind that sits behind any habit. The idea builds on a 1985 paper by psychologist Thomas Borkovec, who suggested that worry may be what keeps anxiety going, rather than only a result of it. The loop has three parts.

  1. Trigger: a feeling of anxiety or uncertainty, or a thought about something that could go wrong.
  2. Worry: replaying possibilities, imagining worst cases, analyzing what might happen, rehearsing how you would cope.
  3. Reward: worrying feels productive. It gives you something to do, and it can bring a short sense of relief or control.

The trouble is that worrying does not settle the uncertainty. Picturing a threatening outcome over and over keeps the threat switched on. The relief fades, the anxiety comes back, and that becomes the trigger for the next round. Each pass teaches your brain that worrying is what to do when things feel uncertain.

In daily life it looks like this. Someone has a work review on Thursday. On Tuesday night she feels a flutter of dread and starts running through what her manager might say and how she would answer. For a few minutes it feels like preparing. By midnight she is more wound up than when she started, and nothing about Thursday has changed.

Why worrying feels like it helps

Anxiety comes with an uncomfortable push to do something. When the problem is in front of you, doing something is the right call: if the car will not start, you phone for help. Most anxiety is about the future, and the future cannot be solved today. The push to act has nowhere to go, so the mind finds it a job, which is worrying. A job feels better than sitting with not knowing. Feeling better for a minute is not the same as solving anything, and that gap is what keeps the habit going. The relief is real. It just does not last, and it costs more anxiety later.

What to do when you catch yourself worrying

You will never be fully sure how things will turn out, and more thinking will not change that. What you can practice is feeling unsure without answering it with worry.

  1. Notice it. Name what is happening, silently or out loud: "I'm feeling uncertain, and my brain is starting to worry." Naming puts a little space between you and the habit.
  2. Get curious. What set this off? What am I doing in response? Is the worry helping me solve anything today?
  3. Act or allow. If there is a real step you can take, take it, or put it on your calendar. If there is nothing to do yet, let the uncertainty sit there without trying to think your way out of it.
  4. Come back. Notice how the anxiety feels in your body, with interest instead of alarm. Then bring your attention back to what is in front of you.

Back to Tuesday night. She catches the flutter and tells herself, "My brain is starting to worry." She asks whether there is anything she can do tonight, and there is one thing: she writes down the two points she wants to make on Thursday. After that there is nothing left to do, so she lets the not-knowing sit there, notices the tightness in her chest, and goes back to the show she was watching.

The takeaway

Expect to do this many times. Over time you are teaching your brain a different response: uncertainty does not have to mean worry.

When worry is more than a habit

Seeing anxiety as a habit is one useful way in, and it fits many people. It is not the whole picture for everyone. Anxiety disorders are real and treatable, and therapy, medication, or both help many people. If worry is taking over your days, your sleep, or your work, or if it comes with panic attacks, a past trauma, or a low mood that will not lift, talk with a clinician instead of managing it alone.

A note on medication

An SSRI can lower the overall volume of worry when it is severe, which makes the four steps easier to practice. Benzodiazepines are not prescribed for daily or long-term use at Alice Tran Psychiatric Care, because they weaken the learning that practice builds.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is worrying ever useful?

Thinking a real problem through once, and ending with a first step you can take, is useful. Worry is the version that circles the same what-if without ending anywhere. The 48-hour question sorts them: if you can do something about it in the next two days, plan the step; if not, it waits.

How is this different from the fear ladder in the anxiety guide?

Exposure targets avoidance of situations and sensations. The habit-loop approach targets the mental habit of worrying. Most people with anxiety benefit from both, and the general anxiety guide on this site covers worry time, problem-solving on paper, and the ladder.

How long until the four steps feel natural?

Many people notice the loop faster within a couple of weeks and feel the pull of worry weaken over a few months of catching it. It is repetition, not insight, that changes the habit.

Can I do this alongside therapy or medication?

Yes. It fits inside CBT and acceptance-based therapy, and it works alongside an SSRI.
