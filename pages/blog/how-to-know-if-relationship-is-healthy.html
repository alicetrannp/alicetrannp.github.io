How Do I Know If My Relationship Is Healthy? Signs, Red Flags, and the Science

Written & medically reviewed by Alice Tran, PMHNP-BC  ·  August 2026  ·  9 min read

It is one of the most common questions people quietly ask themselves, usually late at night, after a good day or a hard one. Is this normal? Is this good for me? Is this what love is supposed to feel like?

The reassuring news is that relationship science has actually studied this, across tens of thousands of couples. Healthy relationships do not mean conflict-free or effortless. They have a recognizable shape, and so do unhealthy ones. Here is how to tell the difference.

What a healthy relationship actually looks like

When researchers pooled data from 43 long-term studies and used machine learning to find what most reliably predicts a happy relationship, the strongest signals were not grand romantic gestures. They were steady, everyday things: feeling that your partner is committed, feeling appreciated, being satisfied with your sex life, sensing that your partner is happy too, and having relatively low conflict.

Day-to-day research adds another layer. Emotional closeness, positive feelings, and good communication each lift relationship satisfaction, and they buffer one another, so that on a rough day closeness can protect the bond when something else is off.

In plain terms, here are the green flags:

  • You feel respected. Your opinions, your boundaries, and your “no” are taken seriously.
  • Communication feels safe. You can raise a problem without bracing for punishment, contempt, or the silent treatment.
  • There is real trust. You do not feel monitored, and you do not have to monitor them.
  • You feel appreciated. Effort gets noticed, and thanks flows both ways.
  • You can be yourself. The relationship makes your world bigger rather than smaller. Friendships, interests, and goals survive.
  • Repair happens after conflict. Disagreements occur, but you find your way back to each other.
  • Closeness feels good, not costly. Intimacy, emotional and physical, is mutual and wanted.

Notice what is not on that list: never fighting. Healthy couples argue. What sets them apart is how they treat each other while doing it, and whether they reconnect afterward.

The quiet signs of an unhealthy relationship

Unhealthy dynamics are not always dramatic. Often they are a slow erosion, a growing sense that you are smaller, more anxious, or more alone than you used to be. Watch for patterns like:

  • Contempt and criticism aimed at who you are, not just what happened.
  • Feeling controlled over your time, money, phone, friendships, or choices.
  • Walking on eggshells, editing yourself to avoid a reaction.
  • Isolation from friends, family, or support.
  • Keeping score, stonewalling, or chronic dishonesty.
  • Your world shrinking while their needs always come first.

Research on relationship warning signs has found that the number and frequency of these behaviors genuinely predict worse outcomes over time, including partners who act arrogant or entitled. One or two rough patches are not a verdict, but a steady accumulation is a signal worth taking seriously.

Red flags that are about safety, not compatibility

Some signs are not relationship problems to work on. They are safety issues, and they deserve a different response.

Warning signs strongly linked to intimate partner violence include:

  • Physical harm of any kind, including hitting, pushing, or choking.
  • Fear. Being afraid of your partner, or of what they will do.
  • Threats, humiliation, or intimidation.
  • Coerced or forced sexual activity.
  • Escalating jealousy and control, or any attempt to cut off your breathing. Strangulation is an especially dangerous warning sign.

Psychological abuse, meaning threats, demeaning comments, monitoring, and control, is as real as physical abuse and often comes first. If any of these are present, the healthiest step is not to communicate better. It is to reach out for help.

If Any of These Are Present

The National Domestic Violence Hotline is free, confidential, and available 24 hours a day in more than 200 languages.

Call 1-800-799-7233  ·  Text START to 88788  ·  Chat at thehotline.org
If you are in immediate danger, call 911.

Why some people struggle to tell

If you find it genuinely hard to know whether your relationship is healthy, that is worth understanding with compassion rather than judgment.

Our earliest relationships create a template for what closeness is supposed to feel like, what researchers call attachment style. People who grew up with consistent, responsive care tend to feel comfortable with intimacy and trust. Those who experienced inconsistent, distant, or frightening caregiving are more likely to develop insecure attachment, either anxiously craving closeness while fearing abandonment, or avoidantly keeping distance in order to feel safe.

This matters because insecure attachment can quietly recalibrate your sense of normal. Chaos can feel like passion. Neglect can feel familiar. Being controlled can be mistaken for being loved. Both attachment anxiety and avoidance are also linked to more depression, anxiety, and loneliness, which further blurs the picture. Recognizing your own pattern is not an excuse. It is a map toward choosing and building something healthier. We wrote more about where these patterns come from in why connection feels like home and neglect makes us fear it.

The biology of feeling safe with someone

There is a physiological signature to a good relationship too. When couples feel supported, the body’s stress system settles. In one striking experiment, partners given oxytocin, the brain’s bonding molecule, showed more positive communication and lower cortisol, the stress hormone, during a conflict discussion.

That is the essence of a healthy bond: your partner becomes a safe haven in stress and a secure base you can venture out from. If being around your partner mostly winds your nervous system up rather than helping it settle, your body may be noticing something worth paying attention to.

A simple gut-check

If you want a quick self-assessment, ask yourself honestly:

  • Do I feel safe, physically and emotionally?
  • Do I feel respected and free to be myself?
  • Do I feel appreciated, and do I appreciate them?
  • Can we disagree and repair?
  • Is my life bigger because of this relationship, or smaller?
  • After time together, do I usually feel steadier or more depleted?

No relationship scores perfectly, and one hard season does not define a partnership. But a consistent pattern of “no,” especially on safety, is meaningful.

When to get support

Wanting help is a sign of investment, not failure. Couples therapy can strengthen communication and closeness, and individual therapy can help you understand your own patterns and needs. If safety is a concern, reach out to a domestic violence hotline or a trusted professional before anything else.

A healthy relationship will not be perfect. But at its core it should feel like a place where you are respected, safe, and free to be fully yourself, and where, more often than not, you both leave the room a little lighter than you entered it.
