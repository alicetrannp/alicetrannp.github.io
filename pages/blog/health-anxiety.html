Health Anxiety
when googling your symptoms becomes the symptom

Written & medically reviewed by Alice Tran, PMHNP-BC  ·  August 2026  ·  8 min read

It starts innocently enough. A headache that lasts a little longer than usual. A weird twinge in your side. A mole that looks slightly different than you remember. So you do what most people do: you Google it.

Ten minutes later, you're three pages deep into a medical website, convinced you have a brain tumor, appendicitis, or melanoma. Your heart is pounding. You're reading about survival rates. You check the mole again. You Google it one more time, just to be sure. And then once more after that.

If this cycle sounds painfully familiar, you're not just anxious. You might be caught in a pattern that researchers have given a name: cyberchondria. And understanding how it works is the first step to breaking free from it.

What Is Health Anxiety?

Health anxiety is an intense preoccupation with the idea that you are seriously ill, or that you're about to become seriously ill, even when there's no medical evidence to support it. Everyone worries about their health sometimes, and that's completely normal. Health anxiety crosses a line when the worry becomes persistent, disproportionate, and starts interfering with your daily life.

In the current diagnostic framework (DSM-5), health anxiety falls into two main categories. If someone has significant physical symptoms along with excessive worry about them, the diagnosis is somatic symptom disorder. If the preoccupation is mainly about having or acquiring a serious disease, but physical symptoms are minimal or absent, the diagnosis is illness anxiety disorder. In both cases, the core problem is the same: the anxiety itself becomes more disabling than any underlying physical issue.

People with illness anxiety disorder are easily alarmed by health information. Hearing that someone else got sick, reading a health headline, or noticing a new sensation in their body can trigger a cascade of fear. And here's what makes it especially frustrating: medical reassurance often doesn't help. A doctor says everything looks fine, and you feel better for an hour or a day, but then the doubt creeps back in.

Why Google Makes It Worse

This is where the internet becomes a problem. Online symptom searching feels like a solution. It feels like you're being proactive, gathering information, taking control. But for people prone to health anxiety, it does the opposite.

Research has found a strong, consistent relationship between health anxiety and compulsive online health searching. A meta-analysis of 20 studies found a significant positive correlation between health anxiety and online health information seeking, and an even stronger correlation between health anxiety and cyberchondria. In fact, health anxiety was the single strongest predictor of cyberchondria severity, outperforming even general anxiety, depression, and obsessive compulsive symptoms.

The problem is structural. Search engines aren't designed to reassure you. They're designed to return results, and serious diagnoses generate more content, more clicks, and more search results than benign explanations. When you search "headache won't go away," the algorithm doesn't lead with "tension headache from stress." It leads with brain tumors, aneurysms, and meningitis. Your anxious brain latches onto the worst case scenario, and the cycle begins.

The Reassurance Trap

Here's the cruel irony of health anxiety: the thing you do to feel better (searching, checking, asking for reassurance) is the thing that keeps the anxiety alive.

Researchers describe online health searching in health anxiety as a safety behavior, similar to the checking rituals seen in obsessive compulsive disorder. You search because the uncertainty feels unbearable. You find information that temporarily calms you. But the relief is short lived, so you search again. Each search reinforces the idea that you need to search to feel safe, and the cycle tightens.

This pattern is maintained through something called intermittent reinforcement. Sometimes your search turns up something reassuring ("most headaches are benign"), and you feel relief. Other times it turns up something terrifying ("could be a sign of..."), and your anxiety spikes. This unpredictable mix of relief and fear is the same mechanism that makes slot machines addictive. Your brain keeps pulling the lever because occasionally it pays out.

How to Know If This Is You

Ask yourself:

  • Do you spend more than a few minutes searching symptoms online, and find it hard to stop once you start?
  • After searching, do you feel more anxious rather than less?
  • Do you check your body repeatedly for signs of illness (feeling for lumps, monitoring your heart rate, examining your skin)?
  • Have you been reassured by a doctor but found the reassurance only lasted hours or days?
  • Has your worry about illness led you to avoid certain activities, foods, or places?
  • Or on the flip side, do you avoid going to the doctor entirely because you're terrified of what they might find?

If several of these resonate, it's worth having a conversation with a healthcare professional. Importantly, illness anxiety disorder has two subtypes: care seeking (frequent doctor visits and tests) and care avoidant (avoiding medical care out of fear). Both are driven by the same underlying anxiety.

What Actually Helps

Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) is the most effective treatment for health anxiety and has the best evidence base for both illness anxiety disorder and somatic symptom disorder. CBT for health anxiety works by helping you identify the thought patterns that fuel your searching and checking, testing those thoughts against evidence, and gradually reducing safety behaviors like Googling symptoms.

A key part of treatment involves response prevention: learning to sit with the uncertainty of not knowing rather than reaching for your phone. This is uncomfortable at first, but it's how your brain learns that uncertainty isn't dangerous.

Set boundaries with Dr. Google. You don't have to quit the internet. But setting concrete rules can help. For example: no symptom searching after 8 PM, a five minute time limit on health searches, or designating one trusted medical website (like a major academic medical center) rather than falling down a search engine rabbit hole.

Find a doctor you trust and use them as your source. Instead of consulting the internet, write down your health questions and bring them to your next appointment. A good doctor will take your concerns seriously without ordering unnecessary tests that only feed the cycle.

Medications can help. If CBT alone isn't enough, SSRIs and SNRIs are effective for health anxiety, just as they are for other anxiety disorders. These medications can lower the overall volume of your anxiety, making it easier to engage in therapy and resist the urge to search.

The Bottom Line

If you found this article because you were in the middle of a symptom search spiral, take a breath. What you're experiencing has a name, a well understood mechanism, and effective treatment. The goal isn't to never worry about your health. The goal is to keep that worry from running your life, and to close the browser tab without needing one more search to feel okay.
