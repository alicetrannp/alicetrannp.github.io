Why Do I Keep Choosing Emotionally Unavailable People? The Science Behind the Pattern

Written & medically reviewed by Alice Tran, PMHNP-BC  ·  August 2026  ·  10 min read

You promise yourself the next one will be different. And then, somehow, you are back in the same place, falling for someone who runs hot and cold, who cannot quite commit, who is physically present but emotionally somewhere else. From the outside it looks like a coincidence. From the inside it feels like a curse.

It is neither. There are real, well-studied psychological and neurological reasons that some of us are drawn, again and again, to people who cannot fully show up. And the honest version is more interesting, and more hopeful, than the usual internet verdict of “you just have an anxious attachment style.”

First, a kinder reframe

You are not broken, and you do not secretly want to be hurt. What you are experiencing is a pattern, a learned set of expectations about what love feels like, laid down long before you had any say in it. Patterns are learned, and learned things can be unlearned. That is the whole point of understanding this.

1. Your attachment system learned a blueprint

Our earliest bonds teach us what to expect from closeness. Psychologists call the result your attachment style, an internal working model of whether other people are reliable and whether you are worthy of care.

This is not pop psychology. In a study that followed 705 people from infancy to age 30, the quality of the early mother and child relationship predicted attachment anxiety and avoidance in adulthood, including in their adult romantic relationships. The blueprint is real, and it is durable.

Broadly, insecure attachment shows up in two flavors:

  • Anxious (preoccupied): a deep craving for closeness paired with a fear of abandonment and a need for reassurance.
  • Avoidant (dismissing): discomfort with dependence and intimacy, and a tendency to keep distance.

Here is the part most articles skip: these two styles are magnetically drawn to each other. An anxious person’s pursuit and an avoidant person’s retreat fit together like a lock and key, which is exactly why an anxiously attached person so often ends up with someone emotionally unavailable. The dynamic feels intensely familiar because, at a nervous-system level, it is.

2. Familiar and safe got wired together, even when familiar hurt

Here is the uncomfortable truth: we are not actually drawn to what is good for us. We are drawn to what is familiar. And for many people, familiar love was inconsistent, sometimes warm, often distant, never quite dependable.

When that is your baseline, a stable, available, emotionally present partner can feel oddly boring, suffocating, or lacking in chemistry. Meanwhile someone who keeps you guessing lights up every circuit you associate with love. You are not choosing badly on purpose. You are mistaking familiarity for compatibility, and they are not the same thing.

3. The brain chemistry of almost: intermittent reinforcement

This is the piece that makes the pattern so sticky, and it deserves more than a buzzword.

An emotionally unavailable partner does not give you nothing. They give you just enough, a burst of warmth, a perfect weekend, a glimpse of the person you hoped they were, and then they withdraw. Psychologists call this intermittent reinforcement: unpredictable, occasional rewards. And it is, by a wide margin, the most powerful way to make a behavior stick. It is the same schedule that makes slot machines addictive.

The neuroscience runs deeper than metaphor. Research shows that unpredictable early environments durably alter the brain’s reward system. In studies across species, chaotic and unpredictable early-life care produced lasting changes in reward responsiveness, and the unpredictability of caregiving specifically was a key driver. Because unpredictable, scarce affection was the emotional climate some children grew up in, their reward system may be calibrated to precisely that: the intoxicating high of intermittent, hard-won love. Consistent affection does not spike the same way. The chase does.

So when people say an unavailable partner is addictive, they are not exaggerating. The reward circuitry involved genuinely overlaps.

4. Self-worth: the quiet engine underneath

Attachment is not only a model of other people. It is a model of yourself. Early experiences shape whether you believe you are worthy of consistent, reliable love.

If, deep down, you do not quite believe you deserve someone who chooses you fully, a partner who withholds will feel strangely right. Winning over someone unavailable can even become a way to prove your worth: if I can finally get them to love me, then I am enough. Childhood emotional maltreatment is linked to exactly this cluster: lower self-worth, lower interpersonal trust, and lower romantic-relationship well-being in adulthood. One study traced a path from childhood emotional abuse and neglect through vulnerable narcissism, a fragile and injury-prone sense of self, to love addiction, the compulsive pursuit of love even when it hurts. The person you are chasing is often a stand-in for a verdict on yourself.

5. Fear of intimacy: choosing unavailable people to stay safe

There is a paradox here worth naming, because it flips the usual story. Sometimes we choose unavailable partners not despite our fear of closeness, but because of it.

If real intimacy feels dangerous, if being truly seen risks rejection, then an unavailable partner is, perversely, the safest possible choice. You get the longing and the drama of love without ever having to risk full exposure. The distance protects you.

Research supports this. Childhood emotional abuse predicts adult fear of intimacy, and the path runs through insecure attachment and heightened rejection sensitivity. There is also a specific profile, fearful-avoidant attachment, in which a person genuinely wants closeness and fears it at the same time. If that is you, chasing someone who can never fully arrive is not a failure of judgment. It is a compromise your nervous system negotiated long ago: get close, but not too close. We wrote more about where that wiring comes from in why connection feels like home and neglect makes us fear it.

6. Why the pattern repeats

Put it together and the loop becomes clear:

  • Early relationships set a blueprint for what love feels like.
  • That blueprint makes a certain kind of person feel familiar, often the unavailable kind.
  • Their hot-and-cold behavior delivers intermittent reinforcement, which hooks the reward system hard.
  • Underneath, low self-worth whispers that this is what you deserve, or what you must earn.
  • Fear of intimacy makes the distance feel secretly safer than real closeness.
  • When it ends, the pain confirms the old belief, and the search begins again.

Childhood emotional maltreatment has a small but real and consistent association with poorer romantic-relationship well-being decades later, and it tends to affect your own experience of the relationship even more than your partner’s. The pattern is common. It is not your destiny.

How to actually break the cycle

Awareness is the first crack in the pattern, but insight alone rarely changes deep relational wiring. What helps:

  • Name your attachment style honestly, including the possibility that you are avoidant, or fearful-avoidant, not only anxious. The nuance matters, because the work is different.
  • Get curious about chemistry. When someone unavailable feels electric, pause and ask whether that spark is compatibility or just familiarity firing. Learn to sit with the boring feeling of someone stable long enough to see whether it is actually safety.
  • Work on self-worth directly, not by winning over someone withholding. The belief that you deserve consistent love is built through evidence you give yourself.
  • Consider therapy. This is the part with the strongest evidence. Attachment insecurity is changeable, and clinicians call the goal earned security. Attachment-based approaches such as Emotionally Focused Therapy are well established for improving relationships and building secure bonds, and individual therapy can help you understand and rewrite the blueprint driving your choices.

The goal is not to kill the longing for love. It is to redirect it toward people who can actually meet it, and to slowly teach your nervous system that steady, available, reciprocal love is not boring. It is what safety was supposed to feel like all along. If you are not sure what that looks like, our guide to knowing whether a relationship is healthy lays out the signs.
