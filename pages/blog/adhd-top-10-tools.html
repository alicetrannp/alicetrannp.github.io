ADHD

ADHD: the top 10 tools,
with setup steps

Written & medically reviewed by Alice Tran, PMHNP-BC  ·  October 2026  ·  15 min read

Adults with ADHD have usually read a great deal of advice and found most of it hard to apply. This guide is the shortlist I give patients: ten tools that are concrete, quick to set up, and worth the effort. Each comes with a reason it works, setup steps, and the common ways it fails. They work with medication and therapy, not instead of them.

The short answer

Begin with the first two tools, a single calendar and a single task list, and use them for a month before adding anything, because several later tools depend on them. After that, introduce tools singly, beginning with the problem that is most expensive for you. The standard for any tool is simple: it has to be in front of you at the moment you need it.

What actually goes wrong

Attention is the headline symptom, but the damage on an ordinary weekday comes from executive function: the mental management that holds a request in mind, senses time passing, gets a task started, ranks what matters, switches between tasks, and steadies emotional reactions. In ADHD these are intermittent, and they drop out at the worst moments.

  • Holding information: a request made in the hallway is half gone by the time you sit down. Tools 1, 2, and 6.
  • Sensing time: plenty of time at 8:10, running late at 8:50 for a 9:00 appointment half an hour away. Tools 1, 2, and 5.
  • Getting started: a one-hour form has been waiting six weeks. Tools 4 and 5.
  • Ranking: a dozen easy replies get written while the one that mattered waits. Tool 3.
  • Switching: a glance at the phone becomes forty minutes. Tools 5, 6, and 9.
  • Steadying reactions: one critical remark colors the whole afternoon. Tool 10.

Computer-based brain training has been tested for ADHD and the improvements mostly do not leave the training screen. What does transfer is relocating the job: memory into a calendar and list, timekeeping into timers, decisions into a few rules written once.

Tools 1 and 2: a single calendar and a single task list

The calendar holds anything with a fixed time. The list holds everything else you must do. Between them they handle most of the remembering and planning that ADHD makes unreliable, which is why they outrank every other tool here. If both have failed for you before, the cause was almost certainly fixable, usually a second list on a sticky note or an item that was going to be entered later.

  • Rule one: one system. One calendar, one list, personal and work combined. No sticky notes, no second notebook, no mental "I will remember." Paper someone hands you gets entered and then recycled.
  • Rule two: capture at once. An obligation goes into the system the moment it appears, in three to five words, even if that means pausing a conversation. Entering it later is the same as not entering it.

Deciding what goes where

  • A dental appointment: calendar, with an alert that allows for the drive.
  • A relative's birthday: calendar, repeating yearly, plus a list item dated a week ahead to buy the card.
  • A report due Friday: a list item dated Friday and a two-hour calendar block on Wednesday to write it. A due date alone does not reserve the time.
  • A recurring bill: autopay (tool 8), or failing that a list item dated five days before it is due.
  • "We should have dinner sometime": a list item with the next concrete move, such as texting two possible dates.
  • An idea with no deadline: a separate long-term list, so the main list stays short.

Setup

  1. Choose the system you will actually open daily. Google Calendar with Google Tasks, Apple Calendar with Reminders, Outlook with Microsoft To Do, or a paper planner all work. The feature that matters most is seeing open time and tasks side by side.
  2. Put the task widget on the first screen of your phone. On a computer, keep the task panel open beside the calendar.
  3. Set two default alerts for every event: a day before and an hour before, or longer if you usually need travel time.
  4. Create a second list named Long-term for anything more than two weeks out.
  5. Spend thirty minutes emptying your head and your piles into the system: appointments, bills, deadlines, birthdays, renewals, replies owed. Then recycle the notes you copied.
  6. If work requires its own calendar, display both in one view so there is still one place to look.
  7. Commit to thirty days before judging the system. Each app switch resets the habit.

The morning review, two to five minutes

Open today's calendar with the list beside it. Find the open time, allowing for travel and one empty gap for whatever runs long. Check off yesterday's completed items and give the rest a new slot. Letter anything new (tool 3). Assign your A items to specific times, the hardest in your sharpest hours. Make the review itself a repeating event with an alert.

The weekly review, fifteen minutes

Look at next week and turn anything needing preparation into a dated item. Look two to four weeks out at trips, birthdays, and deadlines, and add the first step of each. Re-letter the full list, delete what no longer matters, move stale C items to Long-term, and split next week's one or two large items into steps (tool 4). An item that rolls over three days running is too big, too vague, or no longer important.

When you stop using it

Everyone does, after an illness, a trip, or a crunch, and returns to forty overdue items. That is the most predictable moment in the life of any system and the one where most people quit. Do not reconstruct the missed days. Sort the overdue pile: delete the dead items, move the undated ones to Long-term. Spend fifteen minutes capturing what is in your head now. Do a normal review the next morning. The reset takes half an hour.

Tool 3: priority letters

Assign A, B, or C as each item is entered. The decision gets made once, while calm, so the morning review takes seconds. Without a ranking, an ADHD brain works in order of whatever is in front of it or whatever is most interesting, which is how a noon deadline loses to a dozen easy emails.

  • A: must happen today or tomorrow, with a real cost if it waits. Gets a time on today's calendar.
  • B: important, with a deadline somewhere inside this week. Fills remaining open time.
  • C: worth doing, low stakes. Batched on a slow afternoon.
  • Long-term: no deadline and not happening in the next two weeks. Reviewed weekly.

Ask three questions in order and stop at the first yes: is there a real consequence past tomorrow; does it need to happen this week; will it realistically happen within two weeks. If everything seems to be an A, ask honestly what happens if it waits two days. One to three A items is a full workday; if they exceed the open time, move one to tomorrow or reduce it to a first step that fits. In the task app, type the letter and a colon before the item and sort by title so the letters cluster.

Tool 4: split large tasks

A large task offers nothing to start on, and starting is the hardest part. A usable subtask begins with a verb, is something you can picture yourself doing, and, for the first one, takes under five minutes. "Taxes" is a category; "locate last year's return in the email folder" is a step.

  • Split anything longer than about an hour and anything that has rolled over three days.
  • If splitting feels like too much, make the only subtask "spend ten minutes listing the steps."
  • If you split it and still did not begin, the first step is too large. Shrink it until it sounds trivial, set a five-minute timer, and do only that.
  • Time estimates in ADHD run short. Double the first guess and check the actual time afterward.

Tool 5: timed work blocks

Work on one thing for a fixed interval with distractions removed, take a real break, repeat. The timer does the timekeeping ADHD cannot, and a break that is only minutes away makes it easier to leave the phone alone now. The classic version is the Pomodoro technique.

  1. Choose one item, ideally a subtask from tool 4, and write it down.
  2. Phone in another room, unrelated tabs closed, a notepad beside you for tool 6.
  3. Set a timer for twenty-five minutes and work only on that item. Stray thoughts go on the pad.
  4. When it rings, stop, even mid-sentence, and take five minutes, timed. An untimed break has a way of becoming forty-five minutes.
  5. After four blocks, take fifteen to thirty minutes.

Twenty-five is only a default. Try fifteen on and three off for dull tasks, forty-five on and ten off for absorbing work, and keep whichever lets you complete the most blocks. A broken block does not count; reset it. Two completed blocks in a day is a genuine win. A physical timer beats a phone timer, because reaching for the phone is how many sessions end before they begin. If fifteen minutes of focus is out of reach most days, say so at your visit, since that is exactly what treatment addresses.

Tool 6: the thought parking lot

Keep a paper pad at hand while working. When an unrelated thought intrudes, write three to five words and return immediately. New thoughts in ADHD arrive with urgency, but the shower mold has been there for months and can wait twenty-five minutes. Writing it down frees working memory without losing the thought, and it prevents the task switch, which is where the time goes.

Paper, not an app, because an app is one tap from everything else. Do not act on the thought, evaluate it, or rank it. At the break, sort the pad: most entries are discarded; real ones go into the list with a letter; anything under two minutes gets done during the break. A worry that keeps returning gets written once with a time attached. If worries crowd out work most days, mention it, because anxiety travels with ADHD and is treatable.

Tool 7: a home base for belongings

  • A drop zone by the door: a tray with edges, a charging dock so the phone wants to live there, a hook for the bag, a basket for things leaving with you tomorrow, within two steps of the entrance. On arriving, everything goes there before anything else. Practice deliberately for two weeks and enlist the household to return strays.
  • Combine phone, cards, and key: a phone case or stick-on wallet holding two or three cards, with the house key tucked inside or clipped to it. One object to track, and it is the object you already check constantly. Enable a screen lock and device finding.
  • Backups: copy every new key immediately and store the spare with a trusted neighbor or in a combination lockbox, never under a mat. Add a Bluetooth tracker to each bag and key ring.

Tool 8: automated bills

Enable autopay the day you sign up for anything recurring. Much of the so-called ADHD tax is made of late fees, interest, and reconnection charges on bills that could have been paid, and automation removes that cost almost entirely.

  • Cards at a minimum of the minimum payment, ideally the full statement balance; utilities, phone, internet, insurance, rent through your bank's bill pay, and loans.
  • List every recurring charge from two months of statements. Set payment dates just after payday. Keep a buffer in checking at least as large as your biggest monthly bill, with a low-balance alert.
  • Add a monthly ten-minute calendar event to glance at statements, because autopay fails silently when a card expires. When a new card arrives, make updating the autopays an A item.
  • Twice a year, spend thirty minutes finding and cancelling subscriptions you no longer use, using your card app's recurring-charge view and the phone's subscription settings.

Tool 9: a quieter environment

Every object in view and every notification is an invitation to switch tasks, and an ADHD brain accepts more of them. Resisting a cue costs effort all day; removing it costs effort once.

  • Clear the desk to what the current task needs. Face a wall. Keep the phone out of reach, ideally in another room.
  • Disable notifications for everything except calls, messages, the calendar, and alerts with a safety or financial role. Use a focus mode during work blocks.
  • Unsubscribe from promotional email in one sitting using your mail app's subscription tool.
  • Set the phone to grayscale or tinted icons with a quick toggle. It is disorienting for a week and then changes which apps you open.
  • Delete the apps built for endless scrolling, or set app limits with someone else holding the passcode, and log out so reinstalling takes real effort.

Tool 10: self-talk

Many adults with ADHD were called lazy or careless for years before anyone offered a better explanation, and the verdict became their own inner voice. That voice is learned, not a symptom, which means it can be revised. Harsh self-talk is not harmless: believing "I always fail" produces shame and the urge to quit, which makes starting harder and hands the thought more evidence.

A replacement thought, from CBT

Catch the negative thought and write it down with how much you believe it. Name the distortion: all-or-nothing, overgeneralizing, labeling, fortune-telling, "should" statements, dismissing the positive, reasoning from feelings. Then write a replacement that is completely true and strong enough to displace the original. "I can't do this" becomes "This is hard, and I have finished similar things before by taking one step at a time." "I mess everything up" becomes "I missed this deadline and need to tell my manager and fix it; most of my deadlines this year were met and my last review was good." Re-rate the original. If belief barely moved, the replacement needs to be truer or stronger.

Distance, from ACT

Acceptance and commitment therapy sets aside whether a thought is accurate and asks whether it is useful now. Say three versions slowly: "I am a failure." "I am having the thought that I am a failure." "I notice I am having the thought that I am a failure." Same words, less grip. Some patients give the inner critic a cartoon face so that its next appearance registers as a familiar nuisance rather than a verdict.

If the thoughts turn dark

Thoughts of not wanting to be alive, or of harming yourself, need attention now. Call or text 988 at any hour. In immediate danger, call 911.

Today

  1. Install a calendar and a task app, put the task widget on your first screen, and enter the next three things you have to do.
  2. Put a forty-five-minute head-emptying session on the calendar this week.
  3. Take the oldest item on your list, split it, and do the first five-minute step today.
  4. Put a pad and pen where you work.
  5. Choose the drop zone and put a charger and a tray there.
  6. Turn on autopay for one bill tonight.
  7. Switch off every notification except calls, messages, and calendar, and try grayscale for a day.

The takeaway

Relocate the work your brain does unreliably into systems that do it for you: one calendar, one list, a timer, a few written rules, and a home base by the door. Start with the first two tools for a month, add one at a time, and when a tool fails, fix the setup rather than blaming yourself.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do these tools replace medication?

No. Medication makes the tools easier to use, and the tools turn medication's benefit into finished tasks. Most adults do best with both, and the evaluation at this practice addresses both.

Which tool first?

The calendar and the list, for a full thirty days. Several other tools plug into them. Then add the tool aimed at your most expensive problem.

Planners have never worked for me. Why would this?

They usually fail for fixable reasons: a second list somewhere, items deferred for later entry, abandonment after the first busy week. The two rules and the reset plan address each one.

Is there a paper option?

Yes. A planner with schedule and task pages in one book works if it travels with you. The trade-off is no alerts and harder capture on the go.

Is harsh self-talk part of ADHD?

It is learned from years of criticism rather than a core symptom, which means it can be unlearned. Tool 10 gives two methods.

If focus, time, and follow-through keep costing you, an evaluation can tell you whether ADHD is part of the picture.
