ADHD

ADHD: the top 10 tools,
with setup steps

Written & medically reviewed by Alice Tran, PMHNP-BC  ·  October 2026  ·  16 min read

A lot of ADHD advice sounds nice and is hard to use. This is the list worth handing out: ten tools that are practical, fairly easy to set up, and make a big difference. Each one comes with why it works, setup steps, and fixes for the ways it usually breaks. They work alongside medication and therapy, not instead of them.

The short answer

Start with tools 1 and 2, one calendar and one task list, and use them for a month, because tools 3, 4, 6, and 8 plug into them. Then add one tool at a time, starting with whichever problem costs you the most. Every tool has to pass one test: will you see it at the moment you need it?

What ADHD does to a day

ADHD gets described as trouble paying attention, but the part that wrecks an ordinary Tuesday is usually executive function: the brain's management skills that hold information in mind, keep track of time, get you started, pick what matters, switch gears, and settle your reactions. With ADHD they are unreliable, and they tend to fail at the worst possible moment.

  • Working memory: your boss asks for the report by Thursday; by the time you are back at your desk, half of what she asked is gone. Tools 1, 2, and 6.
  • Time blindness: at 8:10 you have plenty of time; at 8:52 you are running out the door for a 9:00 appointment 25 minutes away. Tools 1, 2, and 5.
  • Starting tasks: the tax paperwork takes about an hour; you have known this for six weeks. Tools 4 and 5.
  • Prioritizing: you answer twelve easy emails and skip the one that mattered. Tool 3.
  • Switching tasks: a quick look at your phone turns into forty minutes. Tools 5, 6, and 9.
  • Emotional regulation: one critical comment at 10 am and the rest of the day feels ruined. Tool 10.

Brain-training programs have been studied for ADHD, and the gains mostly stay inside the games. What works is moving the job out of your head. Remembering goes to a calendar and a task list. Keeping time goes to timers and alerts. Deciding goes to a few simple rules you set once.

Tools 1 and 2: one calendar, one task list

A calendar holds everything that happens at a set time. A task list holds everything you need to get done. Together they do most of the remembering and planning that ADHD makes unreliable. If calendars and to-do lists have fizzled for you before, try once more: they almost always fail for a fixable reason, like a second list on a sticky note, and the two golden rules below prevent exactly that.

  • Golden rule 1: one system only. One calendar and one task list, work and personal together. No sticky notes, no second notebook, no "I'll remember that one." Paper someone hands you goes into the system and then into the recycling.
  • Golden rule 2: capture everything immediately. The moment a task or event comes to your attention, it goes in, in three to five words, while the other person is still talking if necessary. With ADHD, "I'll add it later" usually means never.

What goes where

  • Dentist, Tuesday at 3:00: calendar, with an alert timed for travel.
  • Mom's birthday: calendar, repeating yearly, plus a task dated a week earlier, "buy card for Mom."
  • Report due Friday: a task dated Friday, plus a two-hour calendar block on Wednesday to write it. A due date by itself does not make time to do the work.
  • Electric bill: autopay (tool 8); if impossible, a task dated five days before it is due.
  • "We should get dinner soon": a task, "text Alex two dates for dinner." Write the next concrete move.
  • "I should start a podcast": the long-term list. Ideas with no deadline clog the main list.

Setting it up

  1. Pick the system you will open every day: Google Calendar plus Google Tasks, Apple Calendar plus Reminders, Outlook plus Microsoft To Do, or a paper planner. The one feature that matters most is seeing your tasks and your open time together.
  2. Put the task widget on your first home screen, and on a computer open the task panel beside the calendar.
  3. Set default alerts once: one day before and one hour before, longer if you usually need travel time.
  4. Make a second list called Long-term for anything that will not happen in the next two weeks.
  5. Do a 30-minute brain dump: every obligation out of your head and your piles. Appointments, bills, deadlines, birthdays, renewals, replies you owe. Then recycle the sticky notes you copied.
  6. If work requires a separate calendar, show both in one view. Two calendars are fine as long as you look in one place.
  7. Use it for 30 days before you judge it. Switching apps feels productive, and every switch resets the habit to zero.

The daily review, 2 to 5 minutes every morning

Open today's calendar with the task list beside it. Find today's open time, leaving room for travel and one empty gap, because something will run over. Clear yesterday: check off what you finished, give the rest a new slot. Letter anything new (tool 3). Schedule your A tasks, hardest one in your best-focus hours. Make "daily review" a repeating event with an alert, so the calendar reminds you to check the calendar.

The weekly review, 15 minutes once a week

Next week's calendar: anything that needs prep becomes a dated task. Two to four weeks out: trips, birthdays, big deadlines; add a task for the first step of each. Re-letter the whole list and delete what no longer matters. C tasks that have sat more than two weeks move to Long-term. Break next week's one or two big items into steps (tool 4). When a task rolls over three days in a row, it is too big, too vague, or no longer important.

When you fall off the system

At some point you will: a sick week, a vacation, a crunch at work, and the app shows 40 overdue tasks. That is the moment most people quit, even though it is the most predictable part of using any system. Do not back-fill. Triage the overdue pile: delete what no longer matters, move anything without a real deadline to Long-term. Brain dump for 15 minutes. Tomorrow morning, do a normal daily review. The whole reset takes about half an hour.

Tool 3: ABC priorities

Give every task a letter when you add it. The letter makes the decision once, while you are calm, so the morning review takes seconds. With ADHD, whatever is in front of you feels urgent and whatever is not barely exists, which is how the report due at noon loses to twelve easy emails.

  • A: must happen today or tomorrow; there is a real consequence if it waits. Goes onto today's calendar in the daily review.
  • B: important, and this week is soon enough. Fills open time once the A's have slots.
  • C: needs doing, low stakes. Done on light days, or batched into a 45-minute "C hour."
  • Long-term list: no deadline, will not happen in the next two weeks. Scanned weekly.

Ask in order and stop at the first yes: is there a real consequence if it waits past tomorrow? Does it need to happen this week? Will you realistically get to it in the next two weeks? If everything is an A, ask literally what happens if it waits until the day after tomorrow. On a normal workday, one to three A tasks is plenty; if they need more hours than today has open, move one to tomorrow with a date or shrink it to a first step that fits. Type the letter and a colon first, "A: Send Dana the Q3 numbers," and sort the list by title so the letters group.

Tool 4: break big tasks down

If "clean the whole house" is on your list, it will not get touched until twenty minutes before your guests arrive. Starting is the hardest part for an ADHD brain, and a big task gives you nothing to start on. A good subtask starts with a verb, you can picture yourself doing it, and the first one takes under five minutes. "Taxes" is a topic; "find last year's return in the email folder called Taxes" is a step.

  • Break down any task that would take more than about an hour, and any task that has rolled over three days in a row.
  • If breaking it down feels like too much, make the only subtask "spend 10 minutes listing the steps."
  • If you broke it down and still did not start, the first step is too big. Shrink it until it sounds silly, "open the tax folder," then set a five-minute timer and do only that.
  • Estimates run short, and ADHD makes that worse. Double your first guess and check how long it took.

Tool 5: the Pomodoro technique

Work on one thing for a set stretch with no distractions, take a real break, and repeat. The timer takes over the time-keeping that ADHD makes unreliable, and knowing a guilt-free break is minutes away makes it easier to leave the phone alone right now.

  1. Pick one task and write it down. A subtask from tool 4 is ideal.
  2. Clear the decks: phone in another room, close every tab the task does not need, notepad beside you for the distraction log.
  3. Set the timer for 25 minutes and work only on that task. If an unrelated thought shows up, write it on the log and go back.
  4. When it rings, stop, even mid-sentence, and take five minutes. Set a timer for the break too; with ADHD, a five-minute break can become 45 minutes of scrolling.
  5. After four rounds, take 15 to 30 minutes.

Twenty-five minutes is a starting point. Try 15 on and 3 off for boring tasks and hard starts, 45 on and 10 off for deep work you enjoy, and keep whichever lets you finish the most rounds. If you break a round, do not count it; reset and start fresh. Many people never make it to the long break; two rounds in a day is a real win and four is a great day. A physical timer beats the phone, since picking up the phone to start a timer is how many focus sessions end before they begin. If you cannot manage even 15 minutes most days, tell your clinician, because that is what ADHD treatment is meant to help.

Tool 6: the distraction log

Keep a pad of paper beside you while you work. When an unrelated thought barges in, write it down in three to five words and go straight back. With ADHD, a new thought arrives feeling like the most important thing in the world; the mold in the shower has been there for months and can wait 25 minutes. Writing it down gets it out of working memory without losing it, and saves you from switching tasks, which is where the time disappears.

Use paper, since a notes app is one tap from everything else. Do not act on it, argue with it, or rank it. At the end of the round, sort each line: most is trash, cross it out; real items go into the task app with a letter; anything needed and under two minutes gets done during the break. If the same worry keeps coming back, write it once and give it a time, "think about this at 5 pm." If worries crowd out work most days, mention it; anxiety often comes with ADHD and is treatable too.

Tool 7: systems for your stuff

  • The landing station: one spot by the door where phone, wallet, keys, and daily things always go. A tray with edges, a charging dock so the phone wants to live there, a hook for the bag, an outgoing basket, a step or two from the door. The rule: the moment you walk in, before anything else, everything goes on the station. Practice it deliberately for two weeks, and ask the people you live with to put strays back.
  • Phone-wallet-key: three separate objects means three chances to leave one behind. A phone case or stick-on wallet holds two or three cards, with the key inside or clipped to it. Now there is one thing to remember, and it is the thing you already check dozens of times a day. Set a screen lock and turn on Find My.
  • Backups and trackers: copy every new key right away and keep a spare with a nearby trusted person or in a combination lockbox, never under the mat. Put a Bluetooth tracker on every bag and key ring.

Tool 8: automatic payments

The moment you sign up for anything with a recurring bill, turn on autopay. Late fees, interest, and shutoff notices are a big part of the "ADHD tax," and autopay is the cheapest way to stop paying it.

  • Credit cards: at least the minimum, so a late fee cannot happen; the full statement balance if you can. Utilities, phone and internet, insurance, rent through your bank's bill pay, loans.
  • List every recurring bill from the last two months of statements. Pick payment dates a few days after payday. Keep a cushion in checking at least as big as your largest monthly bill, with low-balance alerts.
  • Add a monthly 10-minute event, "glance at statements," because autopay fails silently when a card expires. When a new card arrives, make an A task: "update card on autopays."
  • Twice a year, 30 minutes: find and cancel the subscriptions you stopped using. Check your card app's recurring-charges view, and on the phone the Subscriptions settings.

Tool 9: simplify your environment

Every visible object and every buzz is a small invitation to switch tasks, and an ADHD brain accepts more of those invitations. Resisting a cue takes effort all day; removing it takes effort once.

  • Clear the desk down to what today's task needs. Face a wall. Phone out of arm's reach, ideally in another room.
  • Turn off notifications for everything except calls, texts, your calendar, and anything with a safety or money role. Use a Focus mode for work blocks.
  • Unsubscribe from newsletters and promotions in one pass, using your mail app's subscriptions tool.
  • Make your phone black and white, either tinted icons or full grayscale with a quick toggle. It is disorienting for a week, and then it changes which apps you open.
  • Delete the endless-scroll apps, or add friction with app timers while someone you trust holds the passcode. Log out and delete the saved password too.

Tool 10: your thoughts

You cannot have harsh thoughts about yourself all day and have it not affect how you are doing. Many adults with ADHD grew up being told they were lazy or careless, and if nobody had a better explanation, they believed it. By adulthood, "I'm such an idiot" can feel normal and true. That voice grew out of years of struggle and criticism; it is not a core symptom of ADHD, which means you can work on it.

Replacement thoughts, from CBT

Catch the negative thought and answer it with a replacement that meets two conditions: it is 100 percent true, no wishful thinking, and it is strong enough to take over. "I can't do this" becomes "This is challenging, and I've done similar tasks and managed to get through them, one step at a time." "I always mess things up" becomes "I made a mistake this time. I also do pretty well at work, people like me, and I get a lot done." On paper: the situation in one line; the thought, word for word, and how much you believe it; the thinking error (all-or-nothing, overgeneralization, labeling, fortune-telling, should statements, discounting the positive, emotional reasoning); the replacement and how much you believe it; then re-rate the original. If belief barely moved, the replacement is not true enough or strong enough yet.

Getting distance, from ACT

Acceptance and commitment therapy sets aside whether a thought is right or wrong and asks whether it is useful right now. Say each version slowly: "I'm a failure." "I'm having the thought that I'm a failure." "I'm noticing that I'm having the thought that I'm a failure." The words are the same, and the third grips less. Some people picture the inner critic with a ridiculous face filter, so the next time it starts up they can think, "oh, it's him again," and return to what they were doing.

If the thoughts turn dark

If you are having thoughts of not wanting to be alive, or of hurting yourself, reach out now. Call or text 988 any time. If you are in immediate danger, call 911.

Do this today

  1. Install a calendar and a task app, put the task widget on your first home screen, and enter the next three things you need to do.
  2. Put a 45-minute "brain dump" on the calendar for this week.
  3. Find the task that has sat on your list the longest, break it into steps, and do the first five-minute step today.
  4. Put a notepad where you work and write "distraction log" at the top.
  5. Pick a landing station spot and put a charger and a bowl there.
  6. Turn on autopay for one bill tonight.
  7. Turn off every notification except calls, messages, and calendar, and try grayscale for a day.

The takeaway

Move the job out of your head: one calendar, one task list, a timer, a few rules you set once, and a landing spot by the door. Start with tools 1 and 2 for a month, add one tool at a time, and when a tool fails, fix the setup rather than blaming yourself.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do these tools replace ADHD medication?

No. Medication makes the tools easier to use, and the tools make the gains from medication show up in daily life. Most adults do best with both, and the three-visit evaluation at this practice covers both.

Which tool should I start with?

Tools 1 and 2, one calendar and one task list, for a full 30 days. Tools 3, 4, 6, and 8 plug into them. Then add whichever tool targets the problem costing you the most.

I have tried planners before and they never stuck. Why would this be different?

Planners usually fail for a fixable reason: a second list somewhere else, items that were going to be added later, or quitting after the first busy week. The two golden rules and the reset plan address each of those.

Is there a paper version?

Yes. A paper planner with schedule and task pages in one book works, as long as it travels with you. Alerts and capture on the go are the trade-off.

What about the harsh self-talk? Is that ADHD?

It is learned, from years of criticism, not a core symptom, which means it can be unlearned. Tool 10 gives two methods, and the depression and anxiety handouts on this site cover related skills.

If focus, time, and follow-through have been costing you, an evaluation can tell you whether ADHD is part of the picture.
