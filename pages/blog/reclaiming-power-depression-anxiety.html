Depression & Anxiety

It’s time to:
reclaiming your power over depression and anxiety

Written & medically reviewed by Alice Tran, PMHNP-BC  ·  July 2026  ·  8 min read

If you have ever felt like depression or anxiety has taken the wheel, deciding how your day goes, what you can and cannot do, who you see, and how you feel about yourself, you are not alone. Nearly 1 in 10 adults experiences major depression in any given year, and anxiety disorders are even more common. These conditions are powerful. They whisper (and sometimes shout) that you are helpless, that nothing will change, and that you are not in control.

But here is the truth: it is time to take your power back.

That does not mean pretending the struggle is not real. It means learning that depression and anxiety are not who you are. They are conditions that can be understood, managed, and overcome.

How Depression and Anxiety Steal Your Sense of Control

Depression thrives on helplessness. It tells you that nothing you do matters, that effort is pointless, and that the future is bleak. Anxiety, on the other hand, hijacks your sense of safety, convincing you that danger is everywhere and that you cannot cope.

Together, they create a cycle:

  • You feel overwhelmed, so you withdraw or avoid
  • You withdraw, so you lose connection, accomplishment, and joy
  • You lose those things, so depression and anxiety grow stronger

This cycle can make it feel like the disease is running your life. But cycles can be broken.

Reclaiming Power: What the Evidence Says Works

Recovery is not about willpower alone. It is about using strategies that are proven to shift the balance of power back to you.

1. Rebuild Your Belief in Yourself (Self-Efficacy)

Research consistently shows that people who develop greater belief in their ability to manage their emotions and handle difficult situations experience bigger improvements in both depression and anxiety. This is not toxic positivity; it is a skill that grows with practice.

Start here: Set one small, achievable goal each day. Complete it. Notice that you did it. Over time, these small wins rebuild the confidence that depression tried to erase.

2. Activate Your Life (Behavioral Activation)

Depression wants you on the couch. One of the most effective treatments for depression is doing the opposite: gradually re-engaging in activities that bring pleasure or a sense of accomplishment, even when you do not feel like it.

You do not have to feel motivated to act. Action often comes before motivation, not after.

Start here: Make a short list of activities that used to bring you joy or satisfaction. Pick the easiest one. Do it this week, not because you feel like it, but because you are choosing to.

3. Challenge the Narrative (Cognitive Strategies)

Anxiety and depression distort your thinking. They make you believe the worst-case scenario is the most likely one, or that one setback defines your entire future.

Cognitive behavioral strategies teach you to catch these distorted thoughts, examine the evidence, and replace them with more balanced perspectives. This is not about "thinking positive." It is about thinking accurately.

Start here: When you notice a strong negative thought, write it down. Then ask: "What evidence do I actually have for this? What would I say to a friend who told me this?"

4. Practice Being Present (Mindfulness)

Anxiety pulls you into the future. Depression drags you into the past. Mindfulness anchors you in the present moment, the only place where you actually have power.

Mindfulness-based approaches have been shown to reduce symptoms of both depression and anxiety, and may be especially helpful for people who have not responded to other approaches.

Start here: Try five minutes of focused breathing each morning. When your mind wanders (it will), gently bring it back. That act of returning your attention is the exercise. It is you practicing control.

5. Move Your Body

Physical activity is one of the most underused tools for mental health. Exercise has been shown to produce moderate to large reductions in depression symptoms and can also reduce anxiety. You do not need to run a marathon; a daily walk counts.

Start here: Commit to 10 minutes of movement today. Walk, stretch, dance in your kitchen. Build from there.

6. Ask for Help. It Is a Power Move, Not a Weakness

Reaching out for professional support, whether therapy, medication, or both, is one of the most powerful things you can do. Effective treatments exist, including multiple forms of psychotherapy and over 20 antidepressant medications. Combined treatment (therapy plus medication) tends to work better than either alone, especially for moderate to severe symptoms.

Collaborative care, where your treatment team actively monitors your progress and adjusts the plan, significantly improves outcomes.

The Shift: From Passenger to Driver

Reclaiming power over depression and anxiety does not happen overnight. It is not a single dramatic moment. It is a series of small, deliberate choices:

  • The choice to get out of bed and take a walk
  • The choice to challenge a negative thought instead of accepting it as truth
  • The choice to call a therapist, fill a prescription, or tell someone you are struggling
  • The choice to try again after a setback

Each of these choices is an act of defiance against a disease that wants you to believe you have none.

A Final Word

Depression and anxiety are real. They are not character flaws, and they are not your fault. But they are also not your destiny. With the right tools, the right support, and the willingness to take one small step at a time, you can reclaim your life.

It is time to stop letting the disease decide.

It’s time to.

Ready to take the wheel back?

The Evidence Behind This Article

Behavioral activation shows among the strongest effects of first-line psychotherapies for depression. Self-efficacy, the belief in one’s capacity to manage difficult situations, robustly predicts better outcomes in both depression and anxiety treatment. Exercise, mindfulness, and CBT all carry strong evidence for both conditions, and combined psychotherapy plus medication outperforms either alone for moderate to severe depression.

  • Uphoff E, et al. "Behavioural activation therapy for depression in adults." Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 2020.
  • Cuijpers P, et al. Meta-analyses on combined psychotherapy and pharmacotherapy for adult depression. World Psychiatry.
  • Bandura A. "Self-efficacy: Toward a unifying theory of behavioral change." Psychological Review, 1977, and subsequent clinical outcome literature.
  • National Institute of Mental Health: Depression and Anxiety Disorders. www.nimh.nih.gov
