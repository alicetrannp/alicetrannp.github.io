Is Caffeine Making Your Anxiety Worse?
an honest look at coffee, energy drinks, and racing thoughts

Written & medically reviewed by Alice Tran, PMHNP-BC  ·  August 2026  ·  8 min read

You love your morning coffee. It wakes you up, sharpens your focus, and makes the first hour of the day feel almost manageable. But lately you've noticed something. By your second or third cup, your heart is racing. Your hands feel a little shaky. Your thoughts start moving faster, and not in a productive way. You feel on edge, but you're not sure if it's anxiety or just caffeine.

Here's the honest answer: it might be both. And the line between the two is thinner than most people realize.

What Caffeine Actually Does to Your Brain

Caffeine is a psychoactive drug, the most widely consumed one on the planet. It works by blocking adenosine receptors in your brain. Adenosine is a neurotransmitter that builds up throughout the day and makes you feel sleepy and calm. When caffeine blocks those receptors, the calming signal gets silenced. The result: you feel more alert, more focused, and more energized.

But that's only half the story. When adenosine can't do its job, your brain's excitatory systems run with less opposition. Dopamine and norepinephrine activity increases. Your sympathetic nervous system (the fight or flight system) gets a nudge. Your heart rate rises. Your blood pressure ticks up. And if you're someone who already runs on a higher anxiety baseline, that nudge can feel like a shove.

Research in animals has demonstrated that caffeine's anxiety producing effects are specifically linked to its blockade of adenosine A2A receptors in emotion processing regions of the brain, particularly the ventral hippocampus. Genetic variations in the adenosine A2A receptor gene (ADORA2A) have been associated with significantly greater anxiety responses to caffeine, meaning some people are biologically wired to be more sensitive to caffeine's anxiogenic effects.

The Evidence: Caffeine and Anxiety

This isn't just a feeling. The research is clear and consistent.

A meta-analysis of 14 studies involving 546 healthy participants found that caffeine intake significantly increased anxiety, with a large overall effect size. When broken down by dose, low dose caffeine moderately increased anxiety, while high dose caffeine (above 400 mg, roughly four cups of brewed coffee) produced a highly significant increase.

For people who already have an anxiety disorder, the effects are even more dramatic. A systematic review and meta-analysis of studies in patients with panic disorder found that caffeine at doses equivalent to roughly five cups of coffee induced panic attacks in 51.1% of patients, compared to 0% after placebo. Among healthy controls, only 1.7% experienced panic attacks after the same dose. The researchers concluded that caffeine "highly discriminates" panic disorder patients from healthy adults.

A comprehensive review in the New England Journal of Medicine confirmed that caffeine can induce anxiety, particularly at doses above 200 mg per occasion or 400 mg per day, and in people with anxiety or bipolar disorders. The review also noted that individual differences in caffeine sensitivity are large, influenced by genetics and the rate of caffeine metabolism.

Why Energy Drinks Deserve Extra Attention

Coffee gets most of the attention, but energy drinks are a growing concern, especially among younger adults. A single energy drink can contain anywhere from 80 mg to over 300 mg of caffeine, and some of the larger cans pack 400 mg or more. Unlike sipping a cup of coffee over 30 minutes, people often drink energy drinks quickly, delivering a rapid bolus of caffeine to the system.

Add to that the sugar content, the carbonation, and other stimulant ingredients like taurine and guarana (which contains additional caffeine), and you have a recipe for amplified anxiety symptoms: racing heart, jitteriness, rapid breathing, and the unmistakable feeling that something is wrong.

The Vicious Cycle

Here's where caffeine and anxiety create a particularly cruel feedback loop:

Caffeine disrupts sleep, both by increasing the time it takes to fall asleep and by reducing sleep quality, especially when consumed later in the day. Poor sleep makes your nervous system more reactive the next morning. A more reactive nervous system means more anxiety. More anxiety means more fatigue. More fatigue means you reach for more caffeine. And the cycle repeats.

Caffeine withdrawal can also mimic and worsen anxiety symptoms. Quitting caffeine abruptly after habitual consumption can cause headaches, fatigue, decreased alertness, depressed mood, and even flu like symptoms. These symptoms typically peak 1 to 2 days after stopping and can last 2 to 9 days. For anxious people, the physical discomfort of withdrawal can trigger health anxiety, creating yet another layer of worry.

So Should You Quit Coffee?

Not necessarily. Moderate caffeine intake (up to about 400 mg per day for most healthy adults, or roughly three to four cups of brewed coffee) is considered safe for most people and has even been associated with some health benefits. The question isn't whether caffeine is bad. The question is whether caffeine is bad for you, right now, given your current anxiety levels.

Here's a practical approach:

Track the connection. For one week, pay attention to when you consume caffeine and when your anxiety symptoms spike. Many people notice a clear pattern once they start looking for it.

Cut back gradually, not abruptly. If you suspect caffeine is worsening your anxiety, reduce your intake slowly to avoid withdrawal symptoms. Drop by about half a cup every few days.

Set a caffeine curfew. Caffeine has a half life of roughly 3 to 5 hours in most adults, but it can be much longer depending on your metabolism, medications, and liver function. A general rule: no caffeine after early afternoon if sleep is an issue.

Watch for hidden caffeine. Tea, chocolate, some sodas, pre workout supplements, certain pain medications, and decaf coffee (which still contains small amounts) all contribute to your daily total.

Swap strategically. If you love the ritual of a warm drink, try half-caf or switch your afternoon coffee to herbal tea. Small changes can make a meaningful difference without requiring you to give up something you enjoy.

Talk to your provider. If you've cut back on caffeine and your anxiety is still significant, that's important information. It suggests your anxiety may need treatment beyond lifestyle changes. CBT and medications like SSRIs and SNRIs are effective first line treatments for anxiety disorders and work whether or not you drink coffee.

The Bottom Line

Caffeine doesn't cause anxiety disorders. But in people who are predisposed to anxiety, whether genetically, situationally, or through an existing diagnosis, it can pour fuel on the fire. The good news is that this is one of the most modifiable risk factors in anxiety management. You don't need to quit forever. You just need to find the dose that lets you stay alert without tipping you into a state of alarm.
