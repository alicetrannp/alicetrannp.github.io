The addictive voice:
one skill for any addiction

Written & medically reviewed by Alice Tran, PMHNP-BC  ·  October 2026  ·  10 min read

People in early recovery often describe the same experience: a reasonable-sounding thought arrives, in their own voice, explaining why today is a fine day to drink, or why one bet or one video could not matter. The Addictive Voice Recognition Technique, usually shortened to AVRT, is a method for hearing that thought as something other than yourself. It applies to alcohol, drugs, nicotine, gambling, pornography, and any other compulsive pattern, because it targets the appetite rather than the substance. This practice treats it as one tool among several; it combines well with naltrexone and similar medications, with therapy, and with recovery communities.

The short answer

Every thought that keeps even a small door open to future use belongs to the appetite, not to you, no matter how sensible it sounds. The appetite can crave indefinitely but cannot act; it needs your hands and your agreement. The core commitment is permanent and made now: never again, and no reconsidering. Afterward, urges are identified and labeled rather than debated. Two medical exceptions override everything else: heavy daily drinking and regular benzodiazepine use require supervised stopping, and anyone returning to opioids after a break faces a lethal overdose risk at the old dose.

A brief experiment

Before any theory, try this. Decide, seriously, that you will never use again for the rest of your life. Not a reduction, not a pause. Hold the decision for thirty seconds and pay attention to what happens.

Most people report two simultaneous reactions. One is relief, even hope, at the picture of a life without the substance. The other is a jolt of resistance at the word "never," something between dread and panic. The common explanation is mixed feelings, half of you wanting to stop and half not. AVRT reads the experiment differently. The hope was you. The resistance came from a separate system, and the technique names that system the addictive voice. The experiment is diagnostic: on this one topic there are two speakers inside your head, and you have just heard both.

Defining the voice

AVRT gives the addictive voice a precise definition: any thought, feeling, or image that supports, suggests, or preserves the possibility of using again. Blatant cravings qualify. So do the quieter thoughts that merely leave a crack open: "someday, at a celebration," "once this stressful quarter is over," "nobody can promise forever."

The model behind this is simplified but useful. The part of the brain that handles language, values, planning, and everything you call "me" is relatively new. Beneath it sits an older system that runs appetite and survival: hunger, thirst, desire. An addiction captures that older system with a reward far larger than anything it evolved to expect, and from then on the system pursues the reward with the urgency of a survival need. Two conclusions follow. Craving does not mean you are weak or ill; it means a healthy appetite has been miscalibrated. And the craving is not you.

  • The person: language, values, memory of consequences, the capacity to say "never" and mean it, moral judgment, and control of the muscles.
  • The appetite: pure wanting. Remembers only the pleasure, learns nothing from the damage, has no concept of a future, and controls nothing.

How it hides

The appetite has a single disguise: it speaks in the first person. It borrows your vocabulary, your reasoning style, even your humor, so a craving arrives as an ordinary thought: "I could use a drink tonight." The technique is to catch the sentence and change the pronoun. The wanting is real; the speaker is misidentified. "I want to use" becomes "it wants to use."

Listen for it and its range becomes obvious. It can be crude, and it can be sophisticated, citing research and performing self-compassion. Common examples: that an absolute commitment sets you up to fail; that many people learn to moderate; that this month is the wrong time for something so demanding; that nothing truly serious has happened yet; that a six-month trial followed by a reassessment is the mature approach; that abstinence is for people with worse problems; that addiction is a disease, so relapse is to be expected and forgiven. Each of these sounds prudent. Test each against the definition: does it keep a future door open? If so, it is the appetite talking, whatever costume it wears. They all say later, maybe, or someday, and none of them says never, which is the one word the appetite cannot tolerate.

One of its most effective costumes is a belief about yourself. Frameworks such as the disease model can genuinely help some people. Spoken by the appetite, the same framework becomes permission: a person with a disease cannot be expected never to slip. The test is the same for any idea, scientific, spiritual, or psychological. If it preserves the option of using, it is working for the appetite.

What the voice cannot do

For all its persistence, the appetite has no motor control. It cannot lift a glass, strike a lighter, open a website, or drive to a store. It can only want. To accomplish anything physical it has to persuade you to lend it your body. Test this now: move one finger, then invite the craving to move it without your permission. Every past episode of use required your muscles and your consent. The force that felt overwhelming is, mechanically, a persistent request, and the hands have always been yours.

The heart of the technique

Wanting, however intense and however long it lasts, accomplishes nothing without your cooperation.

The commitment

AVRT asks for a single permanent decision, deliberately more absolute than "I will try" or "one day at a time." Stated plainly: I will never use again, and I will not reconsider. Say it with intent. If saying it produces dread or an angry static, that is the appetite reacting to the one word it fears, and the skill lies in distinguishing its distress from your own relief.

Moderation sounds reasonable, but to the appetite "probably never" and "never" are entirely different sentences. As long as any future use remains possible, the appetite has work to do: erode your resolve and wait for an opening. Close the possibility and the work ends. People frequently find that the quiet afterward is the real relief, because the endless internal negotiation was harder than abstinence itself.

The decision also has to be made now. The appetite has no calendar and lives in a permanent present. A quit date in January will feel exactly as difficult when January arrives, and the appetite will then suggest February.

No small exceptions

A single sip, puff, or wager voids the commitment, and the appetite will be ready with a script: you are powerless, so you may as well continue. If a lapse happens, refuse the script. Treat the lapse as a serious broken promise, work out exactly which thought opened the door, and remake the commitment more firmly. The appetite is reading this paragraph too, preparing for that moment.

Afterward: identify, do not negotiate

Once the commitment is made, the ongoing work is small. Urges continue, sometimes vividly, sometimes months later without warning. When one arrives, identify it as the appetite, label it, and continue your day.

Do not argue with it. The moment you begin listing reasons not to use, you have agreed to negotiate, and negotiating with an appetite is exhausting and can run all night. You owe it no reply. Identification is the whole technique: once the voice is seen clearly, it has lost its disguise, and a desire with no disguise and no hands is background noise. If the noise is loud, change the setting physically: leave the room, walk, call someone, as you would step away from a conversation you did not want to have.

Over weeks to months the noise fades, with occasional surges. A surge can be read as useful information: the appetite is present and audible, and you are hearing it from the outside, which is the stronger position.

Where this sits among other approaches

AVRT originated with Rational Recovery, founded by Jack Trimpey, and in its original form it rejects the disease model, support groups, and treatment of any kind. Some people read that version and stop permanently the same day. This practice takes a broader view. The central skill, hearing the appetite as separate from yourself and closing the door completely, works alongside everything else that helps. Naltrexone, buprenorphine, therapy, and recovery communities do not conflict with the commitment, and for many people they are what holds it in place.

Read this before stopping abruptly

Some substances are medically dangerous to stop suddenly. Heavy daily alcohol use and regular benzodiazepine use (alprazolam, clonazepam, lorazepam, and others) can produce withdrawal seizures and, with alcohol, delirium tremens, which can be fatal. Your decision can be absolute today while the actual stopping is supervised and, if needed, tapered. That is physiology, not the appetite. Opioids carry a different hazard: tolerance drops after any period of abstinence, and a return to a previous dose can be a fatal overdose. This is a further argument for treating the no-exceptions rule as non-negotiable, and for keeping a clinician involved and naloxone on hand. If you are in crisis or thinking about suicide, call or text 988 at any hour.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does this apply to gambling, pornography, or vaping?

Yes. The technique addresses the appetite and the voice that speaks for it, not any particular substance. Substitute your own pattern wherever this guide says "use."

Can I use AVRT with naltrexone or other medication?

Yes. Naltrexone reduces craving and the reward from drinking, which quiets the voice and makes the commitment easier to keep. The naltrexone handout on this site explains how it is prescribed.

What if I have relapsed many times already?

Each relapse was arranged by a thought that left a door open. The technique is to find that thought the next time and correct the pronoun. Past relapses are data, not proof of powerlessness.

Can I still attend meetings or see a therapist?

Yes. The original form of AVRT discourages them; this practice does not. Use the skill alongside whatever supports you.

Who should not stop on their own?

Anyone drinking heavily every day, anyone taking benzodiazepines regularly, and anyone whose opioid tolerance has fallen. The decision can be made today while the stopping is supervised.
