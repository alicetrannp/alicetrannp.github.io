When You’re About to Assume the Worst: A Guide to Catastrophizing and Fortune-Telling

Written & medically reviewed by Alice Tran, PMHNP-BC  ·  August 2026  ·  9 min read

Everyone worries sometimes. But when your mind automatically jumps to the worst possible outcome and treats it as if it is already certain, that is a thinking trap. Two common versions are:

  • Fortune-telling: predicting the future negatively (“This test will come back bad,” “I’ll fail,” “It won’t work out”).
  • Catastrophizing: blowing a problem up to its most extreme, disastrous ending (“If this happens, my whole life is ruined”).

These thoughts feel true and urgent, but a feeling is not a fact. The good news: you can learn to catch these thoughts and respond to them differently. Here is how.

Step 1: Notice and name it

When anxiety spikes, pause and label what your mind is doing: “This is catastrophizing” or “This is fortune-telling.” Simply naming the thought as a mental habit, rather than a truth, creates a little distance and takes away some of its power.

Step 2: Slow your body down first

It is hard to think clearly when your body is in alarm mode. Take a few slow breaths, with the exhale longer than the inhale. Ground yourself in the present moment: notice what you can see, hear, and feel right now. The worst-case story is about the future; your body is safe in this moment.

Step 3: Question the thought

Put the scary prediction on trial. Ask yourself:

  • What is the actual evidence for this? What is the evidence against it?
  • How many times have I feared the worst, and it did not happen?
  • What are other possible outcomes, not just the worst one?
  • Realistically, what is the most likely outcome?
  • Would this thought hold up in a court of law, or is it a guess?

Step 4: So what if it did happen?

Sometimes the fear loses its grip when you follow it all the way through. Ask:

  • If the worst did happen, how bad would it truly be, and would it be permanent?
  • How would I cope? What would I actually do?
  • Will this still matter in a week? A year?
  • What would I tell a friend who had this exact worry?

Rating the situation on a scale, instead of “total disaster” versus “fine,” helps you see it lives somewhere in the middle.

Step 5: Replace it with a balanced thought

You are not forcing fake positivity. You are aiming for realistic and fair. For example:

  • Instead of “This is going to be a catastrophe,” try “This is hard, but I have handled hard things before, and I will deal with what actually happens.”
  • Instead of “I know it will turn out terribly,” try “I do not actually know how this will turn out. I will wait for real information.”

Step 6: Watch the behaviors that feed worry

  • Endless internet searching, seeking reassurance over and over, or checking symptoms usually makes anxiety worse, not better. Try setting limits on it. For example, repeatedly checking whether he is online, or texting again and again, tends to feed the anxiety rather than calm it.
  • Avoiding what scares you gives short-term relief but strengthens the fear long-term. Facing things gradually teaches your brain that you can handle them.

A quick tool for the moment

When you catch yourself spiraling, walk through: Notice it, then breathe, then ask “what is most likely?”, then ask “could I cope if the worst happened?”, then choose a balanced thought.

A worked example: the unanswered text from your partner

Here is what these steps look like in real life.

The trigger: You text your boyfriend and three hours go by with no reply.

The spiral (fortune-telling and catastrophizing): “Why isn’t he answering? Something’s wrong. Maybe he’s with another girl. Maybe he’s cheating on me. Maybe he’s losing interest and this relationship is falling apart.” Your chest tightens and you keep staring at your phone.

Now walk through the steps:

  • Notice and name it: “My mind just jumped from ‘no reply’ all the way to ‘he’s cheating.’ This is fortune-telling and catastrophizing. I am treating a guess as a fact.”
  • Slow down first: Put the phone down for a moment. Take a few slow breaths. Notice you are safe right now. Nothing has actually happened. There is just silence, and silence is not proof of anything.
  • Question the thought: “What is the evidence he is cheating? Honestly, none. He just has not replied yet. What is the evidence against it? He has been caring and reliable, and he often goes quiet when he is busy, driving, working, or his phone is dead. What are other reasons he has not answered? He is at work, in a meeting, at the gym, napping, phone on silent, or simply has not seen it. What is most likely? He is occupied and will reply later.”
  • So what if something were wrong? “Even if he were pulling away, I would want to know the truth and talk about it directly, not decide it alone in my head. And if the very worst happened, it would hurt, but I have gotten through hard things before and I would be okay. I do not have to live through a breakup that has not happened.”
  • Replace it with a balanced thought: “A delayed text almost always means he is busy, not that he is betraying me. I will wait for real information and, if I need to, ask him calmly instead of accusing him based on a story I invented.”

The result: The worry does not vanish completely, but it shrinks from “he is cheating and my relationship is over” down to “he has not replied yet, and I will find out why when he does.” That is the goal: not zero anxiety, but a fair, realistic response.

Another example: the vague message from your boss

The same steps work for almost any trigger.

The trigger: Late in the afternoon, your boss sends a short message: “Can you come see me tomorrow morning? We need to talk.” No other details.

The spiral: “I am in trouble. I am probably getting fired. If I lose this job, I will not be able to pay rent, and everything will fall apart.”

Walk through the steps:

  • Notice and name it: “My mind jumped straight to getting fired. This is catastrophizing.”
  • Slow down first: A few slow breaths. Nothing has actually happened yet.
  • Question the thought: “What is the evidence I am being fired? None. The message did not say that. My last review was good, and my boss messages the team like this sometimes. Other reasons? A new project, feedback, a schedule change, or even good news.”
  • So what if it were bad news? “Even if it were criticism, I could listen and make a plan. If the very worst happened, I have found work before and I would figure out the next step.”
  • Replace it with a balanced thought: “I do not actually know why she wants to talk. I will find out the facts tomorrow instead of living through the worst version all night.”

The result: The worry shrinks from “my life is ending” to “I have a meeting tomorrow and I will handle whatever it is.”

Both worked examples apply the same evidence-based cognitive restructuring and decatastrophizing sequence: naming the automatic thought, weighing evidence for and against, generating alternative explanations, following the fear through to coping, and landing on a balanced thought. The relationship example also reinforces the behavioral point about reassurance-seeking and checking behaviors, which tend to maintain anxiety rather than relieve it.

When to get extra help

Reach out to your clinician or a mental health professional if worry is frequent, hard to control, interferes with sleep, work, or relationships, or if you ever have thoughts of harming yourself. Talk therapy, especially cognitive behavioral therapy, is highly effective for this kind of thinking, and it is a sign of strength, not weakness, to ask for support.

If you are having thoughts that life is not worth living, or thoughts of harming yourself, please reach out right now: call or text 988 (Suicide and Crisis Lifeline, 24/7), text HOME to 741741, or go to the nearest emergency room. These thoughts are a symptom that deserves immediate support, and help genuinely works.

Worry that will not switch off is worth talking about.
