Psychosis

Schizophrenia:
a guide for patients and families

Written & medically reviewed by Alice Tran, PMHNP-BC  ·  October 2026  ·  11 min read

This guide is for people living with schizophrenia and the families who support them. It explains what the illness is, why insight is often low, what treatment involves, and the daily steps that keep people well and out of the hospital. It expands on the one-page schizophrenia handout.

The short answer

Schizophrenia is a treatable brain illness that affects perception, thinking, motivation, and memory. Medication taken every day is the strongest protection against relapse and hospitalization, and long-acting injections remove the need to remember a daily pill. Steady sleep, no cannabis or stimulants, and care for heart health protect both the mind and the body. Families help most by sharing one goal, keeping their loved one well, rather than debating the diagnosis.

What schizophrenia is

Schizophrenia is a group of symptoms rather than a single one. It usually begins between the late teens and early thirties, somewhat earlier in men than in women, and it affects a little under 1 in 100 people worldwide.

  • Positive symptoms: experiences added on top of normal perception, such as hearing or seeing things others do not (hallucinations) and fixed beliefs others do not share (delusions).
  • Disorganization: thoughts or speech that are hard to follow, or behavior that does not fit the situation.
  • Negative symptoms: things taken away, such as drive, interest, emotional expression, and the wish to be with people.
  • Cognitive symptoms: trouble with attention, memory, and planning. These often affect work and school the most.

One way to understand psychosis: the brain starts tagging ordinary things as important, a stranger's glance, a license plate, a phrase on the radio, and then builds a story to explain why they matter. From the inside, the story feels completely real. Treatment aims to restore the ability to work, study, and connect, not only to quiet voices.

Schizophrenia is not a split personality, and it is not caused by parenting. Genes, early brain development, and later stresses, including heavy cannabis use, all contribute.

Insight, and staying hopeful

Many people with schizophrenia do not believe they are ill. This is called low insight, or anosognosia. It happens because the illness affects the parts of the brain that monitor the self. It is not stubbornness or denial.

  • Nobody has to be persuaded to use the word schizophrenia. A person who rejects the label but swallows the pills each morning does better than one who agrees with the label and then quits them.
  • Insight often improves with steady treatment. It may never be complete, and treatment can work well even when insight is incomplete.
  • Stay hopeful and realistic. Medication works well for most people, though it is not always pleasant. Many people with schizophrenia work, study, keep close relationships, and live independently.

Treatment has three parts

  • Medication: the foundation. It keeps people out of the hospital and makes the other two parts possible.
  • Body and routine: steady sleep, a fixed wake-up time, regular movement, and health checks protect the mind and the heart.
  • Support: family education, coping tools such as headphones and music, a notebook recording when voices came and what eased them, and help returning to work or school. Cognitive behavioral therapy for psychosis (CBTp) and family education programs reduce symptoms and relapse when combined with medication.

Medication: what to expect

Antipsychotic medication reduces hallucinations and delusions and, most importantly, prevents relapse. In a review of 75 clinical trials, people who stayed on medication had less than half the one-year relapse rate of people who stopped.

  • Side effects can be managed. Report sleepiness, restlessness (an inner urge to keep moving, called akathisia), stiffness, or weight gain early. A dose change, a timing change, or a switch usually helps.
  • Long-acting injections. Several medicines come as an injection given every two weeks to every six months, depending on the medicine. They remove the daily pill, and the care team knows right away if a dose is missed.
  • Clozapine. If two different antipsychotics have not worked well enough, clozapine is the most effective option available. It requires regular blood counts, especially in the first months. Clozapine often causes constipation, which can become dangerous, so tell your prescriber about any change in bowel habits. Smoking lowers the levels of clozapine and some other antipsychotics, so tell your prescriber before quitting or cutting back, since the dose may need to come down.
  • Never stop suddenly. Relapse after stopping often comes weeks to months later, so feeling fine after a missed week does not mean the medication is unnecessary. Plan any change with your prescriber.

Making the medication routine stick

Of everything that affects how this illness goes, taking the medication reliably is the part most fully in your own hands.

  • Load a seven-day pill box every Sunday, ideally with a family member alongside.
  • Pair the dose with something you already do every day, such as brushing your teeth.
  • Set a daily phone alarm, or use a dispenser that messages a relative when a dose is skipped.
  • Ask whether the whole dose can be taken at bedtime.
  • Ask whether a monthly or six-monthly injection could suit you.

Protect your body

On average, people with schizophrenia live about 15 fewer years than the general population, mostly because of heart and lung disease, diabetes, and smoking, and also because of suicide and accidents. Much of this gap is preventable, and steady treatment lowers the risk of early death.

  • Sleep: a run of short nights is often the first domino before a relapse. A fixed wake-up time, seven days a week, is the anchor.
  • Cannabis and stimulants: daily use of high-potency cannabis is strongly linked with psychosis. Cannabis, methamphetamine, and other stimulants can trigger relapse and can push vulnerable people toward lasting psychosis. Alcohol disrupts sleep.
  • Heart health: antipsychotics can raise weight, blood sugar, and cholesterol. Weigh yourself regularly, check blood pressure, and keep lab appointments, usually at the start, a few months in, and then at least yearly.
  • Smoking: quitting is the single biggest health gain. Varenicline (Chantix) is the most effective quit aid for people with schizophrenia, and both varenicline and nicotine replacement are safe with antipsychotics. Because quitting raises the levels of clozapine and some other antipsychotics, tell your prescriber before you quit.
  • Primary care: see a primary care clinician at least once a year.

Ten habits worth building

Check one or two to start with, not all ten.

  • Dose at one fixed hour daily.
  • Fill a weekly pill organizer each Sunday.
  • Get up at a fixed hour, weekends included.
  • Keep the bedroom dark and quiet.
  • Weigh yourself regularly.
  • Measure blood pressure at home with an arm cuff.
  • Walk, even for two minutes, five days a week.
  • Avoid cannabis and stimulants, and limit alcohol.
  • Post the day's plan on a wall calendar.
  • Write down which voices came and what eased them.

Know your early warning signs

Relapse rarely arrives without warning. Most people show the same few changes in the days to weeks before. Write down yours together with family and the care team, and agree in advance on what each person will do.

  • Sleeping much less, or staying up through the night
  • Pulling away from family and friends
  • Becoming more suspicious, tense, or irritable
  • Trouble concentrating, or speech that is harder to follow
  • Voices returning or growing louder
  • Missed doses, or a decision to stop medication

When the signs appear, call your prescriber early. A short-term medication change in the first days often prevents a hospital stay.

For family and friends

Families are often the first to notice a change and the most important source of support. These approaches help keep the relationship strong.

  • Share one goal: keeping your loved one well and out of the hospital.
  • Do not insist on the diagnosis. Begin with what you both already agree on: poor sleep, stress, feeling afraid, trouble concentrating.
  • Ask whether a symptom bothers them, not whether they still see or hear it.
  • Listen without arguing about whether an experience is real. You can acknowledge how frightening a belief feels without agreeing that it is true.
  • Keep them in charge of their own choices as far as safety allows.
  • Keep home calm and low in conflict. Research on home environments finds that frequent criticism and conflict are linked with more relapse, and patient, warm support with less.
  • Look after yourself too. NAMI Family-to-Family is a free eight-session course for families (nami.org). The book I Am Not Sick, I Don't Need Help! by Xavier Amador teaches a practical way to talk with a loved one who does not believe they are ill.

Get help right away if

  • Several nights of little or no sleep, or voices that have become commanding or threatening
  • Stopping medication, or missing it for more than a few days
  • High fever with stiff muscles and confusion while on an antipsychotic: go to the emergency room
  • On clozapine: fever, sore throat, or flu-like illness, since a same-day blood count is needed
  • On clozapine: no bowel movement for several days, belly pain or swelling, or vomiting
  • On clozapine: chest pain, shortness of breath, or a racing heart, especially in the first weeks

In a crisis

Thoughts of hurting yourself or not wanting to be here: call or text 988 any time, or go to the nearest emergency room. If you or someone else is in immediate danger, call 911.

Good to know

  • Report any new repetitive movements of the mouth, tongue, or fingers right away. This can be a side effect called tardive dyskinesia. Catching it early gives the best chance of improvement, and effective treatments are available.
  • Book: Surviving Schizophrenia by E. Fuller Torrey, the most useful single purchase for a family.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is schizophrenia the same as a split personality?

No. Schizophrenia affects perception, thinking, motivation, and memory. It does not create separate personalities. That is a different and much rarer condition.

Does schizophrenia run in families?

Genes play a real part. Having one parent with schizophrenia raises the risk to roughly 1 in 10, compared with a little under 1 in 100 in the general population. Even so, most people with schizophrenia have no parent with the illness, and parenting style does not cause it.

Will medication be needed for life?

Many people do best with long-term treatment, especially after more than one episode. Any decision to lower or stop medication is made together with your prescriber, slowly, with a written relapse plan in place.

Are people with schizophrenia dangerous?

Most people with schizophrenia are never violent, and they are far more likely to be victims of violence than to cause it. Risk rises with untreated psychosis and substance use, which is one more reason steady treatment matters.

Can someone with schizophrenia work or go to school?

Yes. Many people do, especially with steady treatment. Supported employment programs, such as Individual Placement and Support (IPS), help people find and keep competitive jobs.
