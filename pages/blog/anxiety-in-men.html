Anxiety in Men
why it looks like anger, drinking, and working too much

Written & medically reviewed by Alice Tran, PMHNP-BC  ·  August 2026  ·  8 min read

When most people picture anxiety, they imagine someone who looks visibly nervous: fidgeting, avoiding social situations, admitting they feel worried. But for many men, anxiety doesn't look like that at all. It looks like a short temper. A third drink at dinner. Staying at the office until 9 PM every night. Restless irritability that no one, including the man himself, connects to anxiety.

Anxiety disorders are among the most commonly diagnosed mental health conditions in men, yet male specific anxiety remains dramatically underresearched and underrecognized. A systematic review of men's anxiety found a consistent pattern: men commonly experience anxiety through unique profiles, including prominent psychosomatic symptoms, and tend to underreport their experience, especially younger men.

This matters because anxiety that goes unrecognized goes untreated. And untreated anxiety doesn't just simmer quietly. It leaks out in ways that damage relationships, careers, and health.

Why Anxiety Wears a Disguise in Men

The short answer is socialization. From a young age, many men absorb messages that equate emotional vulnerability with weakness. Expressing fear or worry doesn't fit the script. So the anxiety gets channeled into behaviors that feel more "acceptable" or at least more familiar.

Research on masculinity and mental health has found a complex relationship: adherence to norms of toughness can actually be protective against developing anxiety in some contexts, but adherence to emotional restrictiveness (the belief that men shouldn't express vulnerable emotions) is positively associated with anxiety. In other words, the very strategy men use to hide anxiety may make it worse.

The experience of having anxiety, and especially the act of seeking help for it, can feel like a direct violation of masculine identity for many men. This creates significant self stigma and social stigma, both of which keep men from getting treatment.

The Three Masks Anxiety Wears

Irritability and anger. The DSM 5 lists irritability as a core symptom of generalized anxiety disorder. For many men, low grade chronic anxiety doesn't feel like nervousness. It feels like everything and everyone is annoying. A traffic jam becomes infuriating. A partner's question triggers a disproportionate reaction. The fuse gets shorter, and no one, least of all the man, thinks "anxiety." They think "anger problem" or just "stress."

Alcohol use. The relationship between anxiety and alcohol is well documented and follows a predictable pattern. Anxiety symptoms often come first. Alcohol provides temporary relief (the "self medication hypothesis"). But over time, increasing alcohol use makes anxiety worse, and alcohol withdrawal itself produces anxiety symptoms, creating a self perpetuating cycle. Research shows that comorbidity between anxiety disorders and alcohol use disorders is common, with prevalence rates of co-occurring alcohol dependence and anxiety disorders reaching as high as 35.8% in men. Notably, clinical data indicate that in men with generalized anxiety disorder, comorbid substance use disorders are more likely than in women, where comorbidity tends to involve depression and other anxiety disorders instead.

Overworking. Throwing yourself into work can look like ambition or dedication. But for many anxious men, it's a form of avoidance. Staying busy keeps the mind occupied and wards off the creeping dread that arrives during downtime. Workaholism also provides a sense of control, something anxiety constantly threatens to take away. The problem is that overworking fuels exhaustion, disrupts sleep, and eliminates the recovery time your nervous system needs, all of which make anxiety worse.

What Anxiety Actually Feels Like for Men

Research into men's anxiety presentations reveals that psychosomatic symptoms are especially prominent. Rather than saying "I feel anxious," men are more likely to report:

  • Chest pain or heart pounding
  • Headaches
  • Muscle tension, especially in the jaw, neck, and shoulders
  • Stomach problems
  • Trouble sleeping
  • Difficulty concentrating
  • Feeling "on edge" without knowing why

One study examining young men's anxiety related ambulance calls found that the most common presentations were psychosomatic symptoms, alcohol and drug use, and situational stressors. Three distinct patterns emerged: psychosomatic anxiety, anxious substance use, and complex anxiety. Many of these young men were experiencing anxiety crises that looked nothing like the textbook description.

Breaking the Cycle

Recognize the pattern. If you find that your default responses to stress are anger, drinking, or burying yourself in work, consider the possibility that anxiety is the engine driving those behaviors. This isn't about labeling yourself. It's about getting accurate information so you can make better decisions.

Redefine strength. Getting help for anxiety isn't a sign of weakness any more than getting a torn ligament repaired is a sign of weakness. Research consistently shows that men who do seek treatment for anxiety respond just as well as anyone else. The barrier isn't biology. It's the decision to walk through the door.

Talk to a professional. Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) has strong evidence for treating all major anxiety disorders and works well for men. It's structured, goal oriented, and focused on building concrete skills, which tends to appeal to people who are skeptical of "just talking about feelings." SSRIs and SNRIs are effective first line medications if therapy alone isn't enough.

Get honest about alcohol. If you notice that you drink to calm down, to take the edge off, or to sleep, that's a signal worth paying attention to. You don't need to have a "drinking problem" for alcohol to be masking an anxiety problem. Even moderate self medication can prevent you from addressing the root cause.

Move your body. Exercise has genuine anti anxiety effects supported by clinical evidence. It doesn't need to be extreme. Regular moderate activity, such as brisk walking, swimming, or weight training, helps regulate your stress response system and provides a healthy outlet for the physical tension anxiety creates.

The Bottom Line

Anxiety in men is common, often hidden, and highly treatable. It doesn't always look like nervousness or worry. Sometimes it looks like a guy who's always angry, drinks a little too much, and can't stop working. If that sounds familiar, whether it's you or someone you care about, the most important thing to know is that effective help exists. The hardest part is recognizing what's really going on.
