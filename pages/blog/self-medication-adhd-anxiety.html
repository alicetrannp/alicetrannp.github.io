Calmed by Caffeine, Wired by Wine? What Your Self-Medication Habits Reveal About Hidden ADHD and Anxiety

Written & medically reviewed by Alice Tran, PMHNP-BC  ·  August 2026  ·  10 min read

You drink a large coffee and, instead of feeling jittery, you finally feel calm and focused enough to work. Or you get home and the only thing that seems to quiet your racing mind is a glass or two of wine. Maybe it is a cigarette or vape during stressful moments, an energy drink to come down and concentrate, or a familiar late-night scroll-and-snack routine to shut your brain off.

If you have ever searched why caffeine makes you sleepy, why you need alcohol to relax, or why you cannot turn your brain off at night, you may have stumbled onto something important. These everyday habits are not just personality quirks or bad willpower. For many undiagnosed adults, they are unconscious attempts to regulate a nervous system that runs on ADHD or chronic anxiety.

This is often called self-medication: using easily available substances to manage symptoms that have not yet been named or treated. This post looks at what these patterns can reveal, without judgment, and why they are worth a professional conversation rather than a source of shame.

The Coffee Clue: Why Stimulants Can Calm an ADHD Brain

For most people, caffeine is an upper that increases energy and alertness. But many adults with ADHD describe the opposite: coffee makes them feel calmer, more settled, and better able to focus, sometimes even sleepy. Why?

ADHD involves under-activity in brain systems that use dopamine and norepinephrine to regulate attention and impulse control. Stimulants, including caffeine, boost the signaling in those systems, which is exactly why prescription stimulants are the most effective ADHD treatment. Caffeine specifically acts on the brain’s adenosine system, which is tightly linked to dopamine regulation, and in ADHD research it has been shown to help normalize dopamine function and improve attention.

This shows up in real-world data. A large study of over 1,200 U.S. Army soldiers with adult ADHD found they used significantly more caffeine pills, energy drinks, and other caffeinated beverages than their peers, and that higher caffeine use was associated with fewer ADHD symptoms, suggesting many were unconsciously using it as a form of self-treatment.

So if a cup of coffee makes you feel focused and relaxed rather than wired, it is a clue worth paying attention to. A paradoxical calming response to stimulants can be a sign that your brain is dopamine-hungry in the way an ADHD brain often is. One important caveat: this is a clue, not a home diagnostic test, because caffeine affects everyone differently.

The Racing Brain That Will Not Shut Off

A lesser-known but very real feature of adult ADHD is mental restlessness, or racing thoughts. Research shows that self-reported racing thoughts are not just a bipolar or anxiety symptom. They are an integral, often overlooked feature of adult ADHD, and they are especially likely to intensify in the evening and at bedtime, where they strongly correlate with insomnia and anxiety.

This explains a common pattern: you are fine enough to push through the day, but the moment you lie down, your brain floods with unfinished tasks, replayed conversations, and tomorrow’s worries. If nobody has explained why this happens, the search for an off switch often lands on whatever is legal and available, whether that is alcohol, cannabis, food, or endless scrolling.

The Wine Wind-Down: Alcohol and Nicotine as Self-Medication

Using a substance to quiet an overactive or anxious mind is one of the most common and most under-recognized signs of untreated ADHD and anxiety. Over half of adults diagnosed with ADHD also have a substance use disorder, and a leading explanation is that their use begins as an attempt to self-medicate and cope with their difficulties, long before it ever looks like a problem.

The numbers are striking. Up to 43% of people with ADHD develop an alcohol use disorder over their lifetime, and among adults being treated for alcohol problems, roughly 20% to 33% have ADHD that is largely under-recognized and under-treated. Anxiety plays a powerful role in this pathway. Research shows that anxiety and depression actually mediate the link between ADHD symptoms and drinking and smoking, meaning the emotional distress of undiagnosed ADHD is often what drives the reach for alcohol or nicotine.

A few patterns that may signal self-medication rather than simple habit:

  • The substance targets a symptom. Alcohol to silence racing thoughts, nicotine to focus or steady yourself, caffeine to concentrate rather than to wake up.
  • The relief is specific and reliable. You do not just like it. You feel you need it to function, sleep, or feel normal.
  • It is tied to internal distress. Use spikes with overwhelm, restlessness, or anxiety, not just social occasions.

None of this means you have an addiction or that you are doing something wrong. It means your nervous system may be asking for regulation it is not getting, and there are far more effective, sustainable ways to provide it.

Why This Cycle Is Worth Taking Seriously

Self-medication can work just well enough to keep the underlying condition hidden, while quietly making things worse. Alcohol fragments the very sleep that a racing ADHD brain already struggles with, nicotine dependence deepens, and caffeine crashes fuel anxiety. And because the coping strategy masks the symptoms, the actual treatable condition, whether ADHD, an anxiety disorder, or both, goes unnamed for years.

There is also a reassuring flip side. Properly treating ADHD does not fuel addiction. A large meta-analysis found that treating childhood ADHD with stimulant medication neither increases nor decreases the risk of later substance problems, countering a common fear. And getting the underlying diagnosis right often reduces the drive to self-medicate in the first place, because the symptom being chased finally gets addressed directly.

Anxiety, ADHD, or Both? Why an Accurate Evaluation Matters

These conditions overlap heavily, and self-medication blurs the picture further, so getting the target right matters. Adults with ADHD have roughly 5 times the odds of an anxiety disorder and about 4.5 times the odds of a substance use disorder compared to adults without ADHD. Sorting out what is driving your habits takes more than a quick quiz.

  • Lifelong patterns vs. recent stress. ADHD symptoms trace back to childhood, before age 12, even if they were masked. Situational anxiety often maps onto specific stressors.
  • What the substances are doing for you. Whether use is recreational or targeting focus, sleep, or a racing mind is clinically meaningful.
  • Look-alike conditions. Thyroid problems, depression, sleep disorders, and medication effects can all mimic ADHD and anxiety and need to be ruled out.

Validated tools like the Adult ADHD Self-Report Scale can help structure this, but they are a starting point for a clinical conversation, not a diagnosis on their own.

If your coffee calms you, your wine is the only thing that quiets your mind, or you have been quietly leaning on substances just to feel normal, you are not weak or lacking discipline. Your nervous system may be trying to regulate something that deserves real attention.
