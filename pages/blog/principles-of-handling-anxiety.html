Anxiety

Principles of handling anxiety:
the tools that work

Written & medically reviewed by Alice Tran, PMHNP-BC  ·  October 2026  ·  14 min read

Anxiety can make ordinary days exhausting, and it is also one of the most treatable problems in mental health. This guide explains what anxiety is, why it keeps coming back, and the tools that therapists who specialize in anxiety teach their patients. The tools take practice, and they work for a lot of people.

The short answer

Anxiety is an alarm that goes off too easily. Avoiding what sets it off brings quick relief and teaches the alarm to fire sooner next time. The way out is the opposite: face what you avoid in small steps, cut back on checking and reassurance, give worry a scheduled slot, and calm the body daily. Medication can lower the volume so the practice is possible; it does not replace it.

An alarm that goes off too easily

Anxiety is your body's alarm system, and its job is to protect you. A car swerves into your lane and, before you have time to think, your heart pounds and you jerk the wheel. That is anxiety working as designed. The trouble starts when the alarm goes off for an email from your boss, an unopened bill, or a thought like "what if something happens to my kids?" Your body reacts as if you are in danger while you sit at the kitchen table. When that happens all day, it wears you down: on edge, sleeping badly, snapping at people you love. Some people have felt this way so long they think it is their personality. It is not, and it usually gets better with the right approach.

What anxiety does to your body

When the alarm fires, adrenaline gets the body ready to fight or run. Each feeling has a job, and none of them is dangerous:

  • Racing mind: attention jumps to anything that could be a threat, so nothing else gets through.
  • Pounding heart: blood is going to the muscles, the same as on a steep hill.
  • Fast breathing and tingling fingers: extra air for the muscles; breathing faster than you need makes you lightheaded.
  • Tight chest, tense neck and jaw: muscles braced for action. Hours of bracing becomes a headache.
  • Upset stomach: digestion pauses, which produces butterflies, nausea, or a sudden need for the bathroom.
  • Feeling unreal or far away: uncomfortable, harmless, and it passes.
  • Shaky hands and sweating: adrenaline in tense muscles, and the body cooling itself as if about to run.

Anxiety also shows up in thoughts, as worry, and in what you do: putting things off, avoiding, checking again and again, asking whether everything is okay. That last part, what you do when anxious, turns out to matter most for whether anxiety sticks around.

Why anxiety keeps coming back

Think of the last time you avoided something because it made you anxious: a call sent to voicemail, a letter left unopened, an event skipped. What happened to your anxiety right after? It dropped. That relief is what keeps anxiety going. Your brain notices that avoiding made the bad feeling go away, so avoiding becomes its first move next time, and it concludes the situation must have been dangerous and you got out just in time. The alarm fires faster and louder. The more you avoid, the more things feel scary, and the smaller your life gets.

The sneaky kind of avoidance

Some avoidance is easy to spot. Some is harder: you go, and you do something extra to get through it. Therapists call these safety behaviors. Asking "are you sure it's okay?" again and again. Checking the stove, the locks, the bank balance, where the kids are. Rereading an email ten times before sending it. Researching a decision for hours. Only going if a certain person comes along. Staying busy so you never have to feel it. They help in the moment, and when things turn out fine your brain credits the safety behavior, so you feel you need it every time.

What happens when you stay

Anxiety does not climb forever. If you stay in an anxious situation, it rises, peaks, and comes down on its own, even if you do nothing to make it go away. Most people with anxiety have never seen this happen, because they leave before it does. Staying teaches your brain something reassurance cannot: you expected something bad, you stayed, and it did not happen, or it happened and you handled it. That is what calms the alarm over time, and it is why "don't worry, it'll be fine" helps for a few minutes at most.

The way out: face your fears in small steps

The most effective treatment for anxiety uses that idea on purpose. It is called exposure, and it works like getting into a cold pool: jump into the deep end and you climb straight back out; start on the first step, let your body adjust, and go one step further. Exposure is the core of cognitive behavioral therapy for anxiety, and you can start on your own, with a therapist to help if you get stuck.

Consider someone who worries about money constantly even though the bills get paid. To keep the worry down, he avoids looking at his bank account, leaves bills unopened for weeks, and asks his wife several times a week whether they are okay. Every avoidance helps for a moment, and every month the worry grows.

Step 1: list what you avoid

Write down the situations you avoid and the safety behaviors you use. Be specific. "Money stuff" is too vague to practice; "open my credit card bill and read the whole thing" is something you could do tonight.

Step 2: rate each one

Next to each item, write a number from 0 to 100 for how anxious you would feel doing it. Zero is calm. Fifty is uncomfortable but doable. One hundred is the most anxious you can imagine. The numbers only need to be good enough to put the list in order.

Step 3: build the ladder

Sort from easiest to hardest. His ladder ran from looking at the bank balance for ten seconds (20), to opening one bill and reading the total (30), reading the whole credit card statement (40), going a whole day without asking his wife (50), writing down last month's spending (60), calling the card company about a charge (70), and making a budget together (85). If there is a big jump between steps, add one in between: a shorter version, an easier time of day, or doing it with someone before doing it alone.

Step 4: pick a starting step

Start around 30 to 50: uncomfortable and still doable. Too high and you will bail out partway, which teaches your brain the situation really was dangerous. Too low and you learn nothing.

How to practice a step

  • Before: write one sentence about what you are afraid will happen, and how sure you are, from 0 to 100 percent. "I'll find out we're in serious trouble and be too upset to do anything tonight. 70 percent." Writing it down matters, because afterward it is easy to forget how sure you were.
  • During: do the step and stay with it. Read the whole statement, line by line. Do not close it halfway, and do not go ask whether everything is okay. Notice the anxiety, let it be there, and keep going. For a one-minute step, repeat it several times in a row. For a longer one, stay until you have found out whether the fear came true.
  • After: write what happened. "The bill was higher than I wanted, and it was a normal month. I was upset for a few minutes, then I made dinner." Re-rate the original fear. The gap between what you expected and what happened is what teaches your brain.

Practice most days, at least three or four times a week; once a week is usually not enough for the learning to stick. Keep the same step until it is about half as hard as the first time, then move up. Progress is not a straight line: on a tired, sick, or stressful day a step feels harder. Go down a step for a few days, then keep climbing.

If it is not getting easier

After a couple of weeks on the same step, one of three things is usually the reason. A safety behavior is sneaking in, such as repeating "it's fine, it's fine" the whole time or only practicing with someone present; find it and drop it. The step is too big; break it down and come back. Or you are not practicing often enough; aim for most days.

Cut back on safety behaviors

Checking, asking for reassurance, and over-preparing keep anxiety going the same way avoiding does, and cutting back on them is often an easier place to start than your biggest fears. Write down the ones you use, pick the easiest to give up, and stop it or shrink it in stages: ten balance checks a day becomes five for a week, then two, then one each morning. Expect anxiety to rise at first; that means your brain is finally getting the chance to learn you are okay without it.

Reassurance is one of the hardest to give up. It helps for a few minutes, then the doubt comes back with a new question, and over time it teaches you that you cannot handle not knowing unless someone else says it is okay. If there is a person you usually go to, talk about it at a calm moment, agree that answering feeds the worry, and plan to cut back together. Decide ahead of time what they can say instead: "I love you, and I'm not going to answer that one, because we agreed answering makes the worry worse. I'll sit with you while it passes." The worry pushes back hard for a week or two, then settles if you both hold the line.

Tools for worry

Worry is the thinking part of anxiety. It feels like solving a problem or preparing for something bad, which is why it is hard to stop, but most worry goes in circles and ends where it started, with nothing solved. It also takes credit it does not deserve: when the feared thing does not happen, part of you feels the worrying prevented it.

  • Ask: can I do something about this in the next 48 hours? If yes, there is a problem to solve; plan the first step and put it on the calendar. If no, there is nothing to solve right now, however long you think about it, and it can wait for worry time. Most people find most of their worries land in the "no" group.
  • Solve problems on paper. Write the problem in one sentence. List at least five possible solutions without judging them. Pick one that is good enough. Plan the first step, with a when and a where. Do it. Check how it went, and pick another solution if needed.
  • Give worry its own time. Pick 15 to 30 minutes at the same time and place each day, at least a couple of hours before bed and not in the bedroom. Start a worry list. When a worry shows up during the day, write it down in a few words, tell yourself "not now, I'll think about this at 6:30," and go back to what you were doing. At worry time, set a timer, run each worry through the 48-hour question, and worry about the rest on purpose. When the timer goes off, stop. The first week feels clumsy; after a couple of weeks many worries no longer seem important by the time their appointment comes.
  • Check worry's track record. During worry time, write what you fear and how likely it feels. Days later, write what happened. In a 2020 study, people with generalized anxiety who tracked their worries this way found about nine in ten did not come true. Seeing that in your own handwriting is far more convincing than being told not to worry.
  • Practice not knowing. Checking, researching, and reassurance are all attempts to feel certain, and certainty never arrives. Build the muscle with small, safe practice: pick a restaurant without reading reviews, make a small choice in under a minute, go out without checking the weather, send an email after one read, let someone else plan the evening, watch a film you know nothing about.

Tools for anxious thoughts

  • Turn the what-if into a prediction. "What if the meeting goes badly?" becomes "I'll stumble over my words and my boss will think I'm bad at my job. 60 percent likely." Now it is something you can look at instead of a cloud of dread.
  • Ask two questions. How likely is this, given the other times I predicted something like it? And if it did happen, how would I cope? Anxiety stops the story at the worst moment; keep it going. An awkward moment, a day or two of embarrassment, and life goes on. A plan for the bad outcome is often more calming than trying to convince yourself it will not happen.
  • Talk to yourself like a good coach. Harshness keeps you tense and scared, not disciplined. "That went badly. What will I do differently next time?" instead of "You idiot." If stuck, say what you would say to a friend in the same spot.
  • Notice the thought without believing it. "I'm having the thought that I'm going to fail." The thought is still there, and now you are watching it rather than being pulled along by it.
  • Call it excitement. Anxiety and excitement feel almost the same in the body. Before a presentation or a first date, calm is a long way off, but excitement is close. In a 2014 study, people who said "I'm excited" before singing, speaking, or a math test did better than people who tried to calm down.
  • When anxiety comes from nowhere, stop asking "what if?" and ask "what happened?" Look over the last day or two. Did someone upset you? Did you agree to something you did not want? Is there something sad or angry you have not let yourself feel? Anxiety sometimes stands in for another feeling, and naming it takes the pressure off.

Tools for a tense body

Use these daily to lower baseline anxiety, or in a hard moment to help you stay where you are. Do not use them to escape: if a breathing exercise becomes something you must do to get through every anxious moment, it has become a safety behavior.

  • Slow breathing with a long breath out. The heart slows a little on each exhale. Breathe in through the nose, take one more small sip of air at the top, and let it all out slowly through the mouth. Five minutes a day. In a 2023 study, a month of this improved mood more than five minutes of daily meditation.
  • Count your breaths to 50, one on the in-breath and two on the out. If you lose count, your mind wandered into a worry; noticing that is the skill. Start again at one. Works in a waiting room, in a queue, or in bed.
  • Relax your muscles. Tense one group for five seconds, release for fifteen to twenty, notice the difference. Hands, arms, shoulders, face, stomach, legs.
  • Move your body. Twenty to thirty minutes of something that raises your heart rate on most days. Exercise also gets the body used to a fast heart and heavy breathing, so those feelings are less frightening when anxiety causes them. Check with your doctor first if you have a heart condition.

Everyday things that turn anxiety up

  • Sleep: a bad night makes the next day more anxious, and anxiety makes the next night worse. A fixed wake time every day, weekends included, is the single most helpful change. The insomnia guide on this site has the full plan.
  • Caffeine speeds the heart and feels a lot like anxiety. Cut back slowly over a week or two, especially after noon. Energy drinks, soda, and some teas count.
  • Alcohol calms anxiety for a few hours and then returns it stronger the next morning, with worse sleep. If you drink heavily every day, do not stop suddenly on your own; withdrawal can be dangerous. Talk to a clinician first.
  • Cannabis, especially strong products and edibles, can trigger anxiety and panic, and regular use worsens anxiety over time.
  • News and the phone: hours of scary news, true crime, and scrolling teach the brain the world is dangerous. For one week, check the news once a day at a set time and keep the phone out of the bedroom.

Where medication fits

An SSRI such as sertraline or escitalopram is the usual first medicine. Early side effects, often nausea or feeling more keyed up, settle in a week or so; benefit takes several weeks, sometimes eight to twelve. Medication lowers the volume so that exposure and the tools above become possible, and the two together work better than either alone. Benzodiazepines can calm a crisis, but they weaken the learning that exposure builds and carry dependence risk. At Alice Tran Psychiatric Care they are not prescribed for daily or long-term use.

Your first two weeks

  • Week 1: start a daily worry time and a worry list. Write down what you avoid and the safety behaviors you use. Practice slow breathing five minutes a day. Notice how much caffeine you have and start cutting back if it is a lot.
  • Week 2: turn your list into a fear ladder. Practice your first step at least three or four times. Cut back on one safety behavior. Keep up worry time.

The takeaway

After that, keep climbing the ladder one step at a time and keep the tools that help most. There will be good days and bad days. Many people notice a real difference within a few months of steady practice, and steps that once felt impossible start to feel ordinary.

When anxiety has a specific shape

Some kinds of anxiety have their own patterns and tools: panic attacks, social anxiety, health anxiety, intrusive thoughts and OCD, and worry as a habit. Each has its own guide and handout on this site. If your anxiety began after a traumatic event and comes with nightmares, flashbacks, or always feeling on guard, look for trauma-focused therapy such as prolonged exposure, cognitive processing therapy, or EMDR.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does exposure take to work?

A single step often gets easier within a handful of practices. Working through a whole ladder usually takes several weeks to a few months of practicing most days. Once a week is rarely enough.

Should I start with medication or with these tools?

Both have strong evidence, and together they work better than either alone. If anxiety is severe enough that you cannot engage with the tools, medication first makes sense. If you can engage, start the tools now; an SSRI can be added if progress stalls.

Is it safe to do exposure on my own?

For everyday fears, yes, starting at a step rated 30 to 50. A therapist helps when you keep stalling, when the fear is tied to trauma, or when panic symptoms have not been medically checked. Chest pain, breathlessness that does not settle, or fainting need a medical evaluation first.

What if my worries are about real problems?

Then the 48-hour question sends them to problem-solving on paper rather than worry time. Worry circles a problem; problem-solving goes through it once and ends with a first step on the calendar.

Does Alice Tran Psychiatric Care prescribe benzodiazepines for anxiety?

Not for daily or long-term use. They undercut the learning that exposure builds and carry dependence risk. Occasional, situational use is considered individually.
