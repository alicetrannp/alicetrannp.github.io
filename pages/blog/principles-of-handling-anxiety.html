Anxiety

Principles of handling anxiety:
the tools that work

Written & medically reviewed by Alice Tran, PMHNP-BC  ·  October 2026  ·  13 min read

Most people who come to this practice for anxiety have already tried to think their way out of it. They have reasoned with the fear, distracted themselves from it, and avoided whatever sets it off, and the fear has grown anyway. That is not a failure of willpower. It is how the threat system behaves when it is managed the wrong way. This guide lays out the principles that reliably shrink anxiety, the same ones used in cognitive behavioral therapy, in the order I would teach them in the office.

The short answer

Anxiety persists because of what we do to escape it. Short-term relief, whether from avoiding, checking, or being reassured, teaches the threat system that the danger was real. The treatment principles reverse that: practice the feared situations in graded steps and stay until the prediction fails, give worry a fixed appointment instead of an open door, treat solvable problems on paper, and settle the body every day. Medication can lower the baseline enough to make the practice possible.

Principle 1: the threat system is doing its job, badly

The physical side of anxiety is a survival reflex. The brain decides something is dangerous and the body prepares to run or fight before conscious thought has finished weighing in. That reflex is why a pounding heart, tight chest, rapid breathing, churning stomach, trembling hands, sweating, and a strange sense of unreality all arrive together. None of them signals harm. The heart is pumping blood to the legs; the lungs are taking in extra oxygen; digestion has paused; the muscles are braced. An hour of bracing becomes a headache or a sore jaw. Fast breathing produces tingling and lightheadedness. The feeling of being detached is a byproduct of high arousal and fades on its own.

The reflex misfires when it is triggered by things that cannot hurt you: a message from a supervisor, a bank statement, an unfamiliar symptom, a thought about a child's future. The body responds as if the threat were physical. People who have lived this way for years often describe it as "just how I am." It is not a personality. It is a system that has been trained to fire too readily, and systems can be retrained.

Principle 2: relief is the trap

Here is the mechanism that keeps anxiety alive. When you step away from something frightening, the fear drops within seconds. That drop feels like proof that stepping away was the right move. The brain records two lessons: avoidance works, and the situation must have been dangerous. Next time, the alarm sounds earlier and louder, and the range of things that feel unsafe widens. Over months the pattern narrows a life: fewer places, fewer conversations, fewer decisions made without a second opinion.

The same mechanism operates through smaller moves that let you stay in a situation while never fully facing it. Clinicians call them safety behaviors: re-reading a message many times before sending it, confirming repeatedly that the door is locked, asking a partner whether everything is all right, only attending an event with a particular friend, keeping busy so the feeling never has to be felt. Each one produces relief, and each one quietly credits itself for the fact that nothing went wrong. The evidence that you would have managed without it never gets collected.

Reassurance from other people works the same way and fades the fastest. A kind "it will be fine" lowers the fear for a few minutes, then the doubt returns with a new question attached. Over time, being reassured teaches a person that uncertainty is unbearable without someone else to settle it.

Principle 3: fear has a ceiling, and it comes down if you stay

Anxiety in a situation rises, reaches a peak, and then declines on its own, even when nothing is done to lower it. Most anxious people have never watched that curve complete, because they leave at the peak. Staying through the peak teaches the threat system something no amount of reassurance can: the prediction was wrong, or the feared thing happened and was survivable. This is the single most important idea in anxiety treatment, and every practice exercise below is a way of arranging to experience it.

Principle 4: practice in graded steps

Structured practice is called exposure, and it is the core of cognitive behavioral therapy for anxiety. The approach is a staircase, not a leap. Take a teacher who, after one difficult parent meeting, began dreading every email from a parent, left them unread for days, and asked colleagues to review every reply. Her practice plan looked like this.

  • Inventory. She listed each thing she avoided and each safety behavior, phrased concretely enough to do on a given day: "open a parent email the hour it arrives," "send a reply without a colleague reading it," "go a full day without asking the office whether a complaint came in."
  • Ratings. Beside each item she wrote an anxiety estimate from 0 to 100, with 0 meaning calm and 100 meaning the worst she could imagine. The numbers only need to be good enough to sort the list.
  • Order. Sorted from easiest to hardest, the list became her staircase. Where two steps were far apart, she inserted an intermediate one by shortening the task, choosing a quieter time of day, or doing it once with support before doing it alone.
  • Starting point. She began with a step in the middle range, uncomfortable but achievable. Beginning too high invites an early exit, which teaches the wrong lesson; beginning too low teaches nothing.

Running a practice step

Each step is an experiment with a prediction attached. Before: write the feared outcome as a sentence that could be checked, and estimate how likely it feels. "The parent will be angry and I will not be able to concentrate for the rest of the day, about 70 percent." During: carry out the step without the safety behavior, let the anxiety rise, and remain until you have learned whether the prediction held. For a brief task, repeat it several times in a row. After: record what a neutral observer would have seen, re-estimate the original prediction, and note the lesson. The distance between the forecast and the result is what retrains the system.

Three or four practices a week is the minimum for the learning to hold; daily is better. Stay on a step until it feels roughly half as hard as the first attempt, then climb. Expect uneven progress. Illness, poor sleep, and stress make steps harder on some days; dropping back one step for a few sessions and then continuing is normal and does not undo earlier gains.

When a step stalls

If two weeks of practice have not made a step easier, look for a hidden safety behavior (silent self-reassurance, practicing only with company), a step that is too large and needs splitting, or a schedule that is too sparse. Address the cause rather than concluding the method has failed.

Principle 5: retire the safety behaviors on purpose

Reducing checking, confirming, and over-preparing is often an easier starting point than the biggest fear, and it pays off quickly. Write down the behaviors, choose the easiest to give up, and either stop it or reduce it in stages across a few weeks. Anxiety rises at first; that rise is the system finally getting a chance to learn that you are safe without the behavior, and it subsides with repetition.

For reassurance that involves another person, make a plan together during a calm moment. Agree that answering the question feeds the worry, decide on a shrinking daily allowance, and script what the other person will say when the allowance is used up. Something like: "We agreed I would not answer that, because answering keeps it going. I will stay with you while it passes." Expect pushback in the first week or two, then a noticeable settling.

Principle 6: worry gets an appointment, not an open door

Worry is the mental arm of anxiety. It mimics preparation, which is why it is so hard to put down, but it circles the same scenario without ever arriving anywhere. Four tools help.

  • Sort by actionability. For each worry, ask whether there is a step you could take in the next day or two. If so, it is a problem to solve. If not, it is a worry to postpone. Most worries fall into the second group.
  • Solve problems in writing. State the problem in a sentence. Brainstorm at least five responses without judging them. Choose one that is adequate rather than perfect. Schedule the first action. Carry it out and review. Writing turns circling into a single pass with an endpoint.
  • Schedule a worry period. Set aside fifteen to thirty minutes at the same time daily, away from the bedroom and well before bed. Keep a running list through the day; when a worry surfaces, jot it in a few words, tell yourself it has an appointment, and return to the task at hand. At the scheduled time, set a timer and go through the list on purpose. Stop when the timer ends. This technique, developed by Thomas Borkovec, shrinks total worry time and demonstrates that worry is more controllable than it feels.
  • Audit the forecasts. Record what you expected and how likely it seemed; days later, record what happened. A 2020 study of people with generalized anxiety found that roughly nine in ten of their logged worries never materialized. Your own log is more persuasive than any statistic.

Principle 7: handle anxious thoughts with precision

  • Convert a vague "what if" into a specific prediction with a probability, so it can be examined.
  • Ask two questions: how probable is this, given past predictions of the same kind, and if it happened, how would I manage it? Anxiety ends the story at the worst frame; continuing the story past that frame is often more calming than arguing the event will not occur.
  • Replace harsh self-talk with the voice of a demanding but fair coach: name what went wrong and what to do differently, without contempt.
  • Create distance from a thought by labeling it as a thought ("I notice I am predicting failure") rather than treating it as a fact.
  • Before a performance, relabel the arousal as excitement. The physiology of the two is nearly identical, and a 2014 study found that people who framed pre-performance nerves as excitement performed better than those who tried to calm down.
  • When anxiety appears without an obvious trigger, look backward rather than forward. A disagreement, an unwanted commitment, or an unacknowledged sadness from the last day or two is often underneath it.

Principle 8: settle the body daily, but not as an escape

Physical techniques lower baseline arousal when used routinely and help you remain in a hard moment when used sparingly. The caution is that a technique used to flee every anxious moment becomes another safety behavior.

  • Extended exhalation. Breathe in through the nose, add a small top-up breath, and release slowly through the mouth. Five minutes a day. A 2023 trial found this pattern improved mood more than an equal period of mindfulness meditation.
  • Breath counting up to fifty, restarting whenever the count is lost. Losing the count is the moment of noticing a wandering mind, which is the skill being trained.
  • Tense and release. Contract a muscle group for five seconds, release for fifteen to twenty, and move through the body. Over weeks, tension becomes noticeable earlier.
  • Aerobic exercise on most days, twenty to thirty minutes. It lowers anxiety directly and makes a fast heartbeat and heavy breathing familiar rather than alarming. Check with a physician first if you have a heart condition.

Principle 9: remove the amplifiers

  • Sleep. Short sleep raises next-day anxiety, and anxiety disrupts the following night. A fixed wake time, including weekends, is the highest-yield change. The insomnia guide on this site covers the rest.
  • Caffeine reproduces the physical signature of anxiety. Taper over one to two weeks rather than stopping abruptly, and avoid it after midday. Energy drinks, sodas, and many teas count.
  • Alcohol calms for an evening and rebounds the next morning, with fragmented sleep in between. Daily heavy drinking should not be stopped abruptly without medical advice, because withdrawal can be dangerous.
  • Cannabis, especially high-potency products and edibles, can provoke anxiety and panic, and regular use raises baseline anxiety over time.
  • Feeds and news. A steady diet of alarming content trains the threat system. Try one scheduled news check per day for a week and keep the phone outside the bedroom.

Where medication belongs

An SSRI such as sertraline or escitalopram is the usual first-line medication for anxiety disorders. Early side effects, typically nausea or a jittery feeling, usually settle within a week or two; the benefit builds over several weeks and can take up to twelve for full effect. Medication lowers the baseline so that practice becomes possible, and the combination of medication and practice outperforms either alone. Benzodiazepines quiet an acute crisis but interfere with the learning that practice produces and carry dependence risk, so this practice does not prescribe them for daily or long-term use.

A realistic first month

  • Days 1 to 7: start the worry period and the running list. Write the inventory of avoided situations and safety behaviors. Begin five minutes of extended exhalation daily. Count your caffeine.
  • Days 8 to 14: rate and sort the inventory into a staircase. Carry out the first step on at least three separate days. Retire one safety behavior.
  • Days 15 to 30: climb one step at a time. Hold the worry period. Add aerobic exercise if it is not already there. Review the forecast audit at the end of the month.

The takeaway

Anxiety shrinks when the threat system is given repeated, well-designed evidence that its predictions are wrong. Graded practice supplies that evidence; scheduled worry, written problem-solving, and daily settling of the body make the practice sustainable. Many people see meaningful change within a few months of consistent work.

Specific forms of anxiety

Panic attacks, social anxiety, health anxiety, intrusive thoughts and OCD, and worry as a habit each follow a recognizable pattern with its own tools. Each has a separate guide and a printable handout on this site. Anxiety that began after a traumatic event, with nightmares, flashbacks, or constant vigilance, is best treated with trauma-focused therapy such as prolonged exposure, cognitive processing therapy, or EMDR.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does graded practice take to work?

An individual step often becomes easier within a handful of attempts. Completing a full staircase typically takes several weeks to a few months of practicing most days. Weekly practice is usually too sparse for the learning to consolidate.

Should I start medication or practice first?

Both carry strong evidence, and the combination is stronger than either alone. When anxiety is severe enough that practice is not feasible, starting medication first is reasonable. Otherwise begin the practice now and add an SSRI if progress stalls.

Is it safe to practice on my own?

For everyday fears, starting at a moderate step, yes. A therapist is valuable when steps keep stalling, when the fear is tied to trauma, or when physical symptoms such as chest pain or fainting have not been medically evaluated.

My worries are about real problems. Does postponing them make sense?

The actionability test routes them. A worry with a concrete next step becomes a written problem-solving exercise with a scheduled action. A worry with no available step is postponed to the worry period.

Does Alice Tran Psychiatric Care prescribe benzodiazepines for anxiety?

Not for daily or long-term use, because they undermine the learning that practice builds and carry dependence risk. Occasional situational use is considered case by case.
