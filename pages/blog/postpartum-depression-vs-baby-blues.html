Baby blues or postpartum depression?
a partner's two-week guide

Postpartum Depression vs. the Baby Blues: What Every New Parent Should Know

Written & medically reviewed by Alice Tran, PMHNP-BC  ·  May 2026  ·  6 min read

You are watching someone you love fall apart in the weeks after your baby arrived, and everyone keeps telling you it is normal. Some of it is. Some of it is not. This guide is written for you, the partner, because you are the one in the room, and you are usually the first to notice.

It is organized around the one line that matters most clinically: the two-week mark.

The short answer

The baby blues affect most new mothers, start within days of birth, and are clearly easing by day fourteen. If your partner is not better by two weeks, is getting worse, cannot sleep even when the baby sleeps, or says anything about not wanting to be here, it is no longer the blues. That is postpartum depression or anxiety, it is common, it is very treatable, and your job is to make the call with her, not to wait it out.

Days 1 to 14: what is normal

Up to four in five new mothers go through the baby blues. Hormones drop sharply after delivery, nobody is sleeping, and the responsibility is sudden and total. In this window, expect:

  • Crying that comes out of nowhere and passes within minutes to an hour.
  • Mood that swings from fine to overwhelmed and back in the same afternoon.
  • Worry about the baby that responds to reassurance and settles.
  • Exhaustion and irritability that improve after a stretch of sleep.
  • Feeling unsure and inadequate, while still connecting with the baby.
  • The overall direction is up. Bad hours, but more good hours each day as the second week goes on.

The two-week line

Circle day fourteen on the calendar. The baby blues do not last past it. If what you are seeing is still there, is there most of the day, or is heavier than it was, the diagnosis has changed, and so has what helps. Rest alone will not fix postpartum depression. Treatment will.

Signs it is postpartum depression or anxiety

Postpartum depression affects more than one in five new mothers, and postpartum anxiety is at least as common and more often missed. Watch for:

  • Sadness or emptiness most of the day, not just in waves.
  • Not sleeping when the baby sleeps, because the mind will not stop, even when exhausted.
  • Anxiety that does not switch off: checking the baby's breathing repeatedly, unable to let anyone else hold the baby, dread that something will happen.
  • Feeling disconnected from the baby, or guilt about not feeling what she expected to feel.
  • Not eating, or no interest in anything that used to matter.
  • Anger, irritability, or snapping that is out of character.
  • Saying she is a bad mother, that the baby deserves better, or that you would all be better off without her.
  • Scary intrusive thoughts about harm coming to the baby that horrify her. These are common in postpartum anxiety and are not a sign she will act on them, but they are a sign she needs help.

Emergency signs: do not wait

Call 911 or go to the emergency room if

She is confused, hearing or seeing things that are not there, has not slept in several days yet seems energized, believes things that are clearly not true, or talks about harming herself or the baby in a way that sounds like a plan rather than a fear. This may be postpartum psychosis. It is rare, it comes on fast, and it is a medical emergency. Do not leave her alone with the baby, and do not wait until morning.

What to say

  • Name what you see, without a verdict. "You have cried every afternoon this week and you are not sleeping. I am worried about you." Not "you seem depressed."
  • Take the weight off the diagnosis. "This is really common and it is treatable. It is not about how much you love him."
  • Make the help concrete. "I will take the 2 a.m. feed with a bottle so you get five hours." Vague offers get declined.
  • Offer to make the call together. "I would like to book an appointment with you tomorrow. I will sit with you while we do it."
  • Do not say: "every new mom feels this way," "you just need to sleep," "at least the baby is healthy," or anything that starts with "you should."

What to do this week

  • Protect one stretch of sleep, four to five hours, every night. Take a feed, or arrange for someone else to. Sleep is the first treatment.
  • Lower the bar at home. Fed, safe, held. Everything else can wait.
  • Get her outside once a day, with the baby, even for ten minutes.
  • Reduce visitors who add work and increase people who bring food and leave.
  • Book the appointment. An initial evaluation at Alice Tran Psychiatric Care is in person in Fairfax or by secure video, and partners are welcome to join. Treatment is usually therapy, medication that is compatible with breastfeeding, or both.
  • Check on yourself. About one in ten fathers and partners develop depression in the first year. Irritability, withdrawal, or burying yourself in work count.

The takeaway

Your partner is not failing, and neither are you. Postpartum depression and anxiety are among the most treatable conditions in psychiatry. The partner who notices, names it kindly, and picks up the phone is doing exactly the right thing.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I tell the baby blues from postpartum depression?

Timing and direction. The baby blues start within a few days of birth, come and go, and are clearly easing by day fourteen. Postpartum depression lasts beyond two weeks, is there most of the day, and is getting worse or not better. Anything beyond two weeks is worth a call.

What should I say to my partner?

Name what you see without judging it, and offer help that is specific. "You have not slept in three days and you cried all afternoon. I am taking the baby tonight so you can sleep, and I would like to call someone with you tomorrow." Avoid "you just need to rest" or "every new mom feels this way."

Can a partner book the appointment?

A partner can call the office, ask questions, and help arrange a visit, and is welcome to join the appointment. The patient needs to agree to be seen. Most people who are struggling are relieved when someone else takes the first step.

Can fathers and partners get postpartum depression too?

Yes. Roughly one in ten fathers develops depression in the first year, often showing up as irritability, withdrawal, or working more. The same two-week guide applies to you.

If it has been more than two weeks, or you are worried now, call. Alice Tran Psychiatric Care sees new parents in person in Fairfax and by secure video across Virginia, Maryland, and Oregon. Schedule an initial evaluation or reach out.
