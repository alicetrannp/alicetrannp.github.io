Why does criticism make me shut down?
fight, flight, freeze, and fawn

Written & medically reviewed by Alice Tran, PMHNP-BC  ·  September 2026  ·  6 min read

Someone gives you feedback, maybe even gentle, reasonable feedback, and something in you slams shut. Your mind goes blank. Your chest tightens. You either freeze, go quiet, over-apologize, or feel a surge of defensiveness you cannot quite explain. And afterward you think: "I know they were not attacking me. So why did my body react like they were?"

That gap, between what you logically know and how your body responds, is the whole point of this article. You are not overreacting or being too sensitive. You are watching an ancient survival system switch on at the wrong moment.

The short answer

Your brain runs social threat through the same circuits as physical threat, and those circuits fire before your thinking brain can weigh in. Shutting down under criticism is the freeze or fawn branch of that system. It is not weakness, it is wiring, and wiring can be retrained.

Your brain treats social threat like physical threat

Humans come equipped with a hard-wired set of automatic defense responses, sometimes called the defense cascade, that fire when the brain detects danger. This system runs through the amygdala and related circuits and activates before your thinking brain has time to weigh in. It was built to keep you alive in front of a predator. The catch: the brain does not neatly separate a physical threat from a social one. Being observed, evaluated, or criticized activates the same threat-detection regions, the amygdala, anterior cingulate, and insula, that respond to danger. To your nervous system, "you are being judged" can register as "you are in danger."

The four responses: fight, flight, freeze, and fawn

The defense cascade is not just fight-or-flight. There is a whole menu, and different bodies default to different responses:

  • Fight. The surge of defensiveness, irritation, or the urge to argue back and justify yourself.
  • Flight. The impulse to escape: change the subject, leave the conversation, avoid the person afterward.
  • Freeze. The deer-in-headlights shutdown. Mind blank, words gone, unable to respond. Importantly, freeze is not passive weakness. It is a state of high internal arousal held on pause, the body braced and ready while outwardly still.
  • Fawn. The appease-and-smooth-over response: instant apologizing, agreeing, people-pleasing to make the perceived threat go away.

If you shut down under criticism, you are most likely a freeze-or-fawn responder. That is not a character defect. It is just the branch of the survival tree your nervous system reaches for first.

Why the reaction can feel too big

Anxiety can be understood as this defensive system firing when it does not need to, a normally protective response showing up out of proportion to the actual situation. And if you have had experiences where criticism, conflict, or disapproval genuinely was not safe, a harsh parent, an unpredictable relationship, past trauma, the system becomes more easily triggered. The brain can get locked into responding to today's minor feedback with yesterday's alarm, replaying the old pattern even when the current danger is small or absent. That is why your body can react as if it is under attack when your mind knows it is not.

What helps

  • Name it in the moment. Silently telling yourself "this is my threat response, not the truth about me" begins to bring the thinking brain back online.
  • Buy time before responding. "Let me think about that and come back to you" is a complete sentence. You do not owe an instant reaction while your system is flooded.
  • Regulate the body first, not the thoughts. Slow exhales, feeling your feet on the floor, unclenching your jaw. These signal safety to the nervous system faster than logic can.
  • Separate the feedback from the verdict. "I did this task poorly" is information. "I am a failure" is the alarm talking. They are not the same.
  • Consider deeper support if it is a pattern. When shutdown around criticism is intense or constant, therapy, especially approaches that work with the body's threat responses, can genuinely retrain the system. When it sits on top of an anxiety disorder, ADHD-related rejection sensitivity, or past trauma, a psychiatric evaluation can clarify what is driving it. This wiring can change.

The goal is not to stop caring about feedback. It is to give the reasoning part of your brain a chance to catch up with the alarm, so criticism becomes something you can hear, not something you have to survive.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does my body react to criticism as if I am in physical danger?

Because your brain does not cleanly separate social threat from physical threat. The automatic defense cascade runs through the amygdala and related circuits and fires before your thinking brain weighs in. Being observed, evaluated, or criticized activates the same threat-detection regions, the amygdala, anterior cingulate, and insula, that respond to physical danger, so "you are being judged" can register as "you are in danger."

What is the fawn response?

Fawn is the appease-and-smooth-over branch of the defense cascade: instant apologizing, agreeing, and people-pleasing to make the perceived threat go away. It sits alongside fight (defensiveness), flight (escaping or avoiding), and freeze (going blank). Fawn is widely used clinically but is less formally established in the primary defense-cascade research than the other three.

Is shutting down under criticism a sign of weakness?

No. Freeze is not passive weakness. It is a state of high internal arousal held on pause, with the body braced and ready while outwardly still. If you go blank under criticism, your nervous system is simply reaching for the freeze or fawn branch first.

Why does my reaction feel so much bigger than the situation?

Anxiety can be understood as this defensive system firing when it does not need to, a protective response showing up out of proportion to the actual threat. If criticism or conflict genuinely was not safe in your past, the system becomes more easily triggered and can respond to today's minor feedback with yesterday's alarm.

How do I stop shutting down in the moment?

Regulate the body before the thoughts: slow exhales, feeling your feet on the floor, and unclenching your jaw all signal safety to the nervous system faster than logic can. Silently naming it ("this is my threat response, not the truth about me") and buying time ("let me think about that and come back to you") bring the thinking brain back online.

Can this be changed, or am I just wired this way?

It can change. When shutdown around criticism is intense or persistent, therapy, especially approaches that work directly with the body's threat responses, can retrain the system over time. When it rides on top of an anxiety disorder or past trauma, treating those helps as well.
