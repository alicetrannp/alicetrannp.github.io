Lithium (Lithobid)

You have been prescribed lithium. This page explains what it is, what to expect, and when to call.

Prepared by Alice Tran, PMHNP-BC, FNP-BC · Alice Tran Psychiatric Care · Updated October 2026

Read the full guide All patient resources

What it is

Lithium is a mood stabilizer, the longest-standing and best-proven treatment for bipolar disorder, and the only one shown to lower suicide risk. It calms episodes of mania and, taken long term, prevents both the highs and the lows from coming back. It is also used to boost an antidepressant that is not working on its own.

Lithium is a natural mineral salt. Exactly how it steadies mood is not fully understood, but it appears to calm overactive signaling between brain cells and to protect them over time. Its effect builds gradually over weeks, not hours. It comes as regular tablets, extended-release tablets, and liquid; it is not habit-forming, and a generic is available.

How to take it

  • Take it with food or milk, at the same times each day, usually once or twice daily.
  • Swallow extended-release tablets whole. Do not crush or chew them.
  • Keep your fluids and salt intake steady. Big changes in either move your level.
  • Dehydration from heat, hard exercise, vomiting, or diarrhea can raise your level. Call us if you are ill.
  • Keep your blood tests on schedule. They guide the dose.

What to expect, and when

  • First 1 to 2 weeks: mania starts to settle. Early side effects such as thirst and a mild tremor show up. Your first blood level is usually checked now.
  • Weeks 2 to 6: we fine-tune the dose to your blood level. Mood becomes steadier.
  • Months onward: the full benefit is prevention, with fewer and milder episodes the longer you stay on it.

Blood tests, and when

Lithium's helpful dose and its toxic dose sit close together, so routine labs keep you in the safe zone. These are not optional.

  • Lithium level: 5 to 7 days after starting or any dose change, then every 3 to 6 months once stable. Have it drawn about 12 hours after your last dose, before the morning dose.
  • Kidney function: before starting, then every 6 to 12 months.
  • Thyroid and calcium: before starting, then every 6 to 12 months.
  • Pregnancy test before starting if you could become pregnant, and a weight check at visits.

Common side effects

Early, first days to weeks: fine hand tremor; increased thirst and urinating more often; nausea or loose stools, which usually ease and improve when taken with food.

Longer term: an underactive thyroid (tiredness, feeling cold, weight gain), which is found on bloodwork and treatable; gradual effects on the kidneys over years, which is why kidney function is monitored; weight gain, acne, or worsening psoriasis in some people; mild memory fog at higher levels.

This is not a complete list of side effects. See the pharmacy handout or the FDA label for the full list.

Call the office the same day if

  • Signs of lithium toxicity: a worse or coarse tremor, vomiting, diarrhea, unsteady walking, slurred speech, confusion, or drowsiness. Hold your next dose and get urgent care; go to the emergency room if severe.
  • Getting dehydrated: vomiting, diarrhea, fever, or heavy sweating can raise your level fast. Call us.
  • Little or no urine, severe swelling, or a very fast or irregular heartbeat.
  • New or worsening thoughts of hurting yourself.
  • Rash, swelling, or trouble breathing (call 911).

Medicines and substances to be careful with

  • NSAID pain relievers (ibuprofen, naproxen): raise your lithium level toward the toxic range. Use acetaminophen (Tylenol) instead, and tell us before any regular use.
  • Blood pressure medicines ending in -pril or -sartan (lisinopril, losartan): raise your level. Safe together, but we check your level more closely.
  • Water pills (hydrochlorothiazide and others): raise your level. We adjust the dose and monitor.
  • Dehydration from vomiting, diarrhea, fever, or heavy sweating: raises your level, sometimes fast. Sip fluids and call us if you are ill.
  • Big changes in salt (crash diets, very low-salt eating): less salt drives your level up. Keep salt intake roughly steady day to day.
  • Big changes in caffeine: can shift your level up or down. Keep caffeine roughly consistent.
  • Alcohol: dehydrates you and worsens mood swings. Limit it.

This is not every interaction. Check with your pharmacist or with us before starting any new medicine, supplement, or over-the-counter product.

What helps it work

  • Taking it consistently, every day. Steady levels are what prevent episodes.
  • Keeping your blood-test and appointment schedule, even when you feel well.
  • Drinking water regularly and keeping salt intake steady, especially in heat or hard exercise.
  • Tracking your mood and sleep. Early changes are easier to catch.
  • Telling every doctor, dentist, and pharmacist that you take lithium before starting anything new.

Quick questions

What happens if I miss a dose? Take it when you remember, unless it is within about 6 hours of your next dose; then skip it. Never double up, since that can push your level toward toxic. If you have missed doses for more than a day or two, call us before restarting; your level may need rechecking.

Will it change my personality? No. Lithium steadies mood swings; it does not flatten who you are. If you feel dull, slowed, or foggy, that is usually a dose or thyroid issue we can address.

What about salt, sports drinks, and hot weather? Keep salt and fluids fairly steady. Sweating a lot, crash dieting, or cutting out salt can raise your level; loading salt can lower it. In heat or hard workouts, hydrate steadily and do not overdo salty electrolyte drinks without asking.

Can I stop once I feel better? Do not stop on your own. Stopping lithium, especially abruptly, sharply raises the odds of another episode. When it is time to stop, we taper slowly over weeks.

Is it safe in pregnancy? Tell us early if you are pregnant or planning pregnancy. Lithium carries a small heart risk to the baby, and the decision is a careful balance made together. Do not stop on your own.

Good to know

  • Keep lithium out of reach of children. An overdose is a medical emergency.
  • Tell us before any surgery or procedure; lithium is sometimes paused around anesthesia.
  • This medication raises serotonin. Read the serotonin syndrome handout (alicetrannp.com/pages/handout-serotonin-syndrome.html) so you know the warning signs.
