Lithium (Lithobid)

You have been prescribed lithium. This page explains what it is, what to expect, and when to call.

Prepared by Alice Tran, PMHNP-BC, FNP-BC · Alice Tran Psychiatric Care · Updated October 2026

Read the full guide All patient resources

What it is

Lithium is a mood stabilizer with the longest track record in psychiatry. It treats episodes of mania, and taken over the long term it prevents both the high and the low phases of bipolar disorder from returning. It is also the only psychiatric medication with solid evidence that it reduces the risk of suicide, and it is sometimes added to an antidepressant that has not worked well enough alone.

Chemically it is a simple mineral salt. Its mechanism is only partly understood; it appears to quiet overactive signaling between nerve cells and to protect them over time. Benefit accumulates over weeks rather than hours. It comes as immediate-release and extended-release tablets and as a liquid, is not habit-forming, and is inexpensive as a generic. The one condition it imposes is regular blood testing, because the dose that helps and the dose that harms are not far apart.

How to take it

  • With food or milk, at the same times daily, usually once or twice a day.
  • Extended-release tablets are swallowed whole, never crushed or chewed.
  • Keep your fluid and salt intake roughly constant. Big swings in either change the amount of lithium in your blood.
  • Heat, hard exercise, vomiting, or diarrhea can concentrate lithium. Drink extra water in those situations and call us if you are ill.
  • Attend every scheduled blood draw. The results, not a fixed number, determine your dose.

What to expect, and when

  • Weeks one and two: mania begins to settle. Thirst, a slight hand tremor, and queasiness often appear. The first blood level is usually drawn in this window.
  • Weeks two through six: the dose is fine-tuned against your level, and mood becomes steadier.
  • Months onward: the main benefit is prevention. Episodes become rarer and milder the longer you stay on it.

Blood tests, and when

Because the therapeutic and toxic ranges sit close together, lab work is what keeps lithium safe. These checks are part of the prescription, not an optional extra.

  • Lithium level: five to seven days after starting and after each dose change, then every three to six months once stable. The sample is drawn about twelve hours after the last dose, before the morning dose.
  • Kidney function: before starting, then every six to twelve months.
  • Thyroid and calcium: before starting, then every six to twelve months.
  • Pregnancy test before starting when relevant, and weight at visits.

Common side effects

Early, first days to weeks: a fine tremor of the hands, more thirst and more frequent urination, queasiness or loose stools that improve with food. Longer term: an underactive thyroid, which shows up on blood tests and is easily treated; gradual effects on kidney function over years, which is why we track it; weight gain, acne, or a flare of psoriasis in some people; a slight mental dullness at higher levels.

This is a summary, not a complete inventory of side effects. The pharmacy leaflet and the FDA label contain the full list.

Call the office the same day if

  • Possible toxicity: a tremor that becomes coarse or clearly worse, vomiting, diarrhea, unsteady walking, slurred speech, confusion, or marked drowsiness. Hold the next dose and seek care the same day; go to the emergency room if symptoms are severe.
  • Fluid loss: a stomach illness, a fever, or a day of heavy sweating can concentrate lithium within hours. Call us.
  • Passing little or no urine, significant swelling, or a very rapid or irregular heartbeat.
  • New or worsening thoughts of self-harm.
  • Rash, facial swelling, or trouble breathing: call 911.

Medicines and substances to be careful with

  • Ibuprofen, naproxen, and other NSAIDs push lithium levels toward the toxic range. Use acetaminophen instead, and tell us before any regular NSAID use.
  • Blood pressure medicines in the -pril and -sartan families (such as lisinopril and losartan) raise lithium levels. They can be used together, with closer level checks.
  • Diuretics (water pills) raise lithium levels. We lower the dose and recheck.
  • Dehydration from illness or heavy sweating concentrates lithium, sometimes rapidly. Keep sipping fluids and call us when you are sick.
  • Sudden salt changes: a crash diet or a very low-salt diet drives the level up. Keep salt intake roughly even from day to day.
  • Caffeine shifts the level in either direction when intake changes sharply. Keep it consistent.
  • Alcohol dehydrates and destabilizes mood. Keep it minimal.

Other interactions exist. Confirm any new prescription, supplement, or over-the-counter product with the pharmacist or with us.

What helps it work

  • Daily consistency. Stable levels are what prevent episodes.
  • Keeping lab and visit appointments even when you feel entirely well.
  • Steady hydration and salt, with extra attention in heat and during exercise.
  • A simple log of sleep and mood, so early changes are visible.
  • Telling every doctor, dentist, and pharmacist about lithium before anything new is prescribed.

Quick questions

I missed a dose. Take it when you remember unless the next dose is within about six hours; then skip it. Never double, because a double dose can push the level into the toxic range. If you have missed more than a day or two, call before restarting, since your level may need rechecking.

Will it change who I am? No. Lithium levels out the swings; it does not flatten personality. Feeling dull, slowed, or foggy usually points to the dose or the thyroid, both of which we can adjust.

Salt, sports drinks, hot weather? Keep salt and fluids steady. Heavy sweating, crash dieting, or cutting salt can raise the level; loading up on salt can lower it. In heat or hard exercise, hydrate steadily and ask before using salty electrolyte drinks regularly.

Can I stop once I feel well? Not on your own. Stopping lithium, especially quickly, sharply raises the chance of another episode. When stopping is appropriate, we taper over weeks.

Pregnancy? Tell us early if you are pregnant or planning to be. Lithium carries a small risk to the baby's heart, and the decision balances that against the risk of relapse. Do not stop on your own.

Dosing and levels, for reference

The dose is set by blood level rather than by a standard number. The figures below are typical ranges; your own label governs.

  • Starting: commonly 300 mg at bedtime, increased by 300 mg every three to seven days as tolerated. Each 300 mg step raises the level by roughly 0.2 mEq/L in a typical adult.
  • Usual total: 900 to 1800 mg daily, often taken entirely at bedtime. A single nightly dose peaks during sleep, which reduces daytime tremor and dullness and is gentler on the kidneys than divided dosing.
  • Long-term target range: 0.6 to 0.8 mEq/L. Keeping the level at or under 0.8 is the single most protective step for the kidneys over the years.
  • Acute mania target: 0.8 to 1.2 mEq/L, for a limited period.
  • Too high: above 1.2 is the toxic range; above 1.5 is an emergency. Some people show toxic symptoms within the normal range, so symptoms count as much as the number.
  • Older adults: often started at 150 mg, raised more slowly, with targets about 30 percent lower.
  • Timing of the draw: twelve hours after the last dose, before the morning dose, and at least five days after any dose change. A level drawn at another time cannot be interpreted.

Monitoring schedule

  • Before starting: kidney function (creatinine, eGFR, BUN), electrolytes, calcium, thyroid (TSH), a blood count, a pregnancy test where relevant, weight and blood pressure, and an ECG for anyone over 50 or with heart history.
  • Five to seven days in: lithium level and kidney function.
  • Three months: lithium level, kidney function, thyroid. Thyroid changes tend to appear between two weeks and six months, so this check should not be skipped.
  • Six and twelve months: lithium level, kidney function, thyroid, weight and blood pressure.
  • Once stable: level and kidney function every six to twelve months; thyroid and calcium yearly.
  • After any dose change or any new interacting medicine: a level about a week later.
  • More frequently over age 65, during instability, on a new interacting drug, or when kidney numbers are drifting.

If a lab comes back abnormal

  • High TSH: thyroid hormone is added and lithium continues. Roughly one person in forty develops an underactive thyroid each year on lithium, and it is treatable.
  • Falling kidney function: the dose comes down, checks become more frequent, and a kidney specialist is involved if needed. Many people remain stable for years at a lower dose. Stopping is not automatic.
  • High calcium: a parathyroid check. About four percent of long-term users develop this, usually without symptoms.
  • Troublesome tremor: a lower dose, less caffeine, or a small dose of propranolol usually resolves it.
  • Thirst and frequent urination: expected, and not a sign of kidney damage. Water rather than sugary drinks; a single bedtime dose often helps.

Tapering, if lithium ever stops

Abrupt discontinuation carries roughly a sevenfold increase in the risk of a mood episode over the following weeks, and some people respond less well if lithium is later restarted. The shortest reasonable taper is thirty days, typically 300 mg per month; after years of use, the taper runs six to twelve months and slows toward the end. Preventing a relapse is far easier than treating one.

Good to know

  • Store lithium out of children's reach. Overdose is a medical emergency.
  • Tell us before any surgery or anesthesia; lithium is sometimes paused around procedures.
  • Lithium raises serotonin activity. Read the serotonin syndrome sheet (alicetrannp.com/pages/handout-serotonin-syndrome.html) so the warning signs are familiar.
