Sertraline (Zoloft)

You have been prescribed sertraline. This page explains what it is, what to expect, and when to call.

Prepared by Alice Tran, PMHNP-BC, FNP-BC · Alice Tran Psychiatric Care · Updated October 2026

Read the full guide All patient resources

What it is

Sertraline belongs to the SSRI family of antidepressants and is one of the first medications prescribers reach for. It is approved for depression, panic disorder, social anxiety, OCD, PTSD, and premenstrual dysphoric disorder, and it is used widely for generalized anxiety. Because it has the longest safety record in pregnancy and is gentle on the heart, it is often the preferred choice for people who are pregnant or who have cardiac disease. It is available as 25, 50, and 100 mg tablets and as a liquid, and the generic costs little.

It works by slowing the removal of serotonin from the gaps between nerve cells, so more of the chemical stays active. That change begins with the first dose, but the mood and anxiety benefit depends on slower adjustments in the brain that unfold over weeks.

How to take it

  • One dose daily, with a meal. Food noticeably reduces nausea and loose stools.
  • Morning is the usual time. If it makes you drowsy, move it to evening and let us know.
  • We generally begin at a low dose and increase gradually, particularly in people prone to panic, so that early restlessness stays mild.
  • A missed dose remembered within a few hours can be taken. Closer to the next dose, skip it. Never take two at once.
  • Allow a fair trial. Six weeks at a working dose is the earliest point at which to judge it.

What to expect, and when

  • First two weeks: the adjustment period. Stomach upset, loose stools, headache, or a wired feeling are common and usually fade. Taking it with food helps.
  • Weeks two through six: digestive effects settle and the first improvements in mood, sleep, and worry appear.
  • Weeks six through twelve: full benefit. Anxiety symptoms tend to be the last to resolve.

Common side effects

Early: loose stools are the effect most characteristic of sertraline; also nausea, headache, restlessness, and disturbed sleep. Ongoing: reduced sexual desire or delayed orgasm, which is common and has solutions, so please mention it. Weight usually changes little. A muted or flattened emotional range generally signals that the dose is higher than needed.

This list is a summary, not a complete inventory of side effects. The pharmacy leaflet and the FDA label contain the full list.

What to keep an eye on

  • Routine blood tests are not needed for most people. The follow-up visits in the first six weeks, and after any dose change, are the monitoring.
  • Sodium: for adults over 65 or anyone taking a diuretic, a baseline level and a recheck about two weeks after starting.
  • Weight and blood pressure at regular visits.

Call the office the same day if

  • Thoughts of suicide or a sharp drop in mood, especially in the first weeks and after dose changes; the risk is greatest under age 25.
  • Agitation or confusion together with fast heartbeat, fever, heavy sweating, or twitching muscles. These can indicate serotonin syndrome; see the attached sheet.
  • Bleeding or bruising that is unusual for you, particularly if you also take ibuprofen, naproxen, aspirin, or a blood thinner.
  • Confusion, severe headache, weakness, or fainting, which can signal low sodium, more often after 65.
  • A stretch of needing almost no sleep with racing thoughts or unusual energy.
  • Hives, facial swelling, or difficulty breathing: call 911.

Medicines and substances to be careful with

  • MAOI antidepressants: never together, and a 14-day gap in either direction.
  • Other serotonin-raising medicines: tramadol, triptan migraine drugs, other antidepressants, St. John's wort, and the cough suppressant dextromethorphan. Each adds to serotonin syndrome risk; tell us before combining.
  • Decongestant pills such as pseudoephedrine and phenylephrine can raise blood pressure and jitteriness. Saline nasal spray is a safe substitute.
  • Ibuprofen, naproxen, aspirin, and anticoagulants raise bleeding risk several-fold with sertraline. Acetaminophen is preferred for pain.
  • Linezolid and methylene blue behave like MAOIs. Any new prescriber or surgeon should know you take sertraline.
  • At higher sertraline doses, around 150 mg and above, blood levels of some heart, ADHD, and antidepressant medications rise. We adjust and monitor when these are combined.
  • Alcohol and new supplements can intensify side effects or interact. Check with us or the pharmacist first.

Other interactions exist. Confirm any new prescription, supplement, or over-the-counter product with the pharmacist or with us.

What helps it work

  • A consistent daily time, with food.
  • Patience through the full six-week trial before deciding.
  • Therapy alongside it. The pair outperforms either one alone.
  • Regular exercise and a steady sleep schedule, which add to the antidepressant effect.
  • Reporting side effects rather than quietly stopping. Nearly all of them can be managed.
  • Never stopping abruptly; we taper together.

Quick questions

I forgot a dose. Now what? A few hours late, take it. Nearly time for the next one, skip it, and do not double. Sertraline clears from the body over roughly a week, so several missed days in a row can bring on dizziness, electric-shock sensations, and flu-like symptoms. Consistency matters.

Will it make me a different person? No. It relieves the symptoms of depression and anxiety and leaves your personality intact; most people say they feel more like themselves. Emotional flatness is a dosing issue to report, not a feature of the drug.

When can I stop? Not abruptly. Sertraline causes milder withdrawal than paroxetine but can still produce dizziness and brain zaps if stopped suddenly. Most people continue six to twelve months after recovery, longer when episodes have recurred, and we taper when the time comes.

What about pregnancy and breastfeeding? Sertraline has more pregnancy data than any other antidepressant and is a first choice while breastfeeding. Untreated depression carries its own risks, so decisions are made together, not by stopping on your own. MotherToBaby (mothertobaby.org) has reliable summaries.

Can I drink? There is no outright ban, but alcohol lowers mood, fragments sleep, amplifies side effects, and undercuts the benefit. Keep it light and tell us what is usual for you.

Stopping

Do not stop without talking to us. We reduce the dose in steps over several weeks and watch for returning symptoms.

Good to know

  • Every prescriber, dentist, and pharmacist you see should know you take sertraline.
  • This medication raises serotonin. The serotonin syndrome warning sheet is attached as the next page of this handout.

Serotonin syndrome: what to watch for

Several medications raise serotonin. Taken together, or at high doses, they can rarely push it too high. This page tells you how to recognize it and what to do.

What it is

Serotonin syndrome happens when too much serotonin builds up in the nervous system. It is uncommon, and most cases are mild, but it can become dangerous within hours. It almost always follows a new medication, a dose increase, or a new combination, usually within the first day.

Medications that raise serotonin

  • Antidepressants: SSRIs, SNRIs, vortioxetine, vilazodone, trazodone, mirtazapine, bupropion, Auvelity, and tricyclics such as doxepin
  • Lithium and buspirone
  • Migraine medicines called triptans, such as sumatriptan
  • Pain medicines tramadol, meperidine, and fentanyl; the cough suppressant dextromethorphan
  • Supplements: St. John's wort, tryptophan, and some weight-loss products
  • Linezolid, an antibiotic, and MAOI antidepressants, which must never be combined with the others

Early signs

  • Restlessness, anxiety, or agitation that feels new
  • Shivering, sweating, or goosebumps without being cold
  • Diarrhea, nausea, or vomiting
  • Fast heartbeat, dilated pupils, or a headache
  • Muscle twitching, especially in the legs, or exaggerated reflexes

Severe signs

  • High fever (over 101°F / 38.5°C)
  • Stiff muscles, severe shaking, or seizures
  • Confusion, hallucinations, or extreme agitation
  • Irregular heartbeat, fainting, or trouble breathing

What to do

  • Severe signs: call 911 or go to the nearest emergency room. Bring a list of every medication and supplement you take.
  • Early signs within a day of a change: call the office the same day at (703) 791-9099. Do not take the next dose of the new or increased medication until you have spoken with us.
  • Do not stop a long-standing antidepressant on your own unless we tell you to; most cases resolve within a day of stopping the newest agent.

Preventing it

  • Keep one current list of everything you take, including over-the-counter cough medicine and supplements, and show it to every prescriber and pharmacist.
  • Ask before adding any new medicine, especially migraine medicines, pain medicines, and cough medicines containing dextromethorphan.
  • Do not take St. John's wort or tryptophan with an antidepressant.
  • Never combine an MAOI with another antidepressant; a 14-day gap is required when switching.
